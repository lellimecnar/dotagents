# Routing unwanted household items

## Purpose
Send every outgoing item to the right destination (sell, donate, recycle, hazardous-waste
drop-off, or trash) quickly and safely, with realistic expectations about resale value and
charity rules. The method is a fixed decision tree plus a locally researched directory that the
household's own skill keeps current. General information, not tax advice.

## When to use
- A decluttering session produces outbound items.
- "Can I donate / sell / trash this?", "Where does this battery/paint/mattress go?"
- Comparing resale platforms, pricing used goods, or checking what charities accept.
- Building or refreshing a local disposal directory for a household.

## Method

### 1. Decision tree (top to bottom, first YES wins)
1. **Is it dangerous?** (flammable, corrosive, toxic, battery, propane, pesticide, aerosol,
   fluorescent bulb, medicine, sharps) -> hazardous route (section 4). Never trash, never drain.
2. **Is it electronic or does it have a cord/battery?** -> e-waste/battery route (section 5). Some
   US states bar landfilling electronics; check the local rule.
3. **Is it a mattress, appliance, tire or paint?** -> special-item route (section 6).
4. **Is it clean, complete, working, safe and to current standards?** If not (stained, broken,
   missing pieces, recalled): recycle if a program exists, else trash. Charities pay to dispose of
   rejects, so do not donate junk.
5. **Is resale value above the effort threshold?** Rule of thumb (judgment): sell only if you will
   net at least about $25 AND it is easy to photograph and hand off, or it is a known higher-value
   category (section 2). Otherwise donate.
6. **Donate** (section 3). Keep the receipt; used clothing and household goods must be in good used
   condition or better to deduct [IRS Pub 526].
7. **Else trash** at the local transfer station or curbside.

**Same-day rule:** load the car and drop it that day. A staging pile near the door is where
donations go to die. (judgment)

### 2. Selling
- Price from **sold** listings, not active ones; median sold price minus ~10% for a fast sale.
- Typical recovery (judgment): most furniture 10-30% of retail; kids' clothing a few dollars per
  piece unless bundled; name brands hold best; particle-board furniture and large TVs often net
  near zero.
- Time cost: one online listing takes ~20-40 min including photos and messages; shipping adds ~20.
  An item under ~$25 is rarely worth it. Batch: photograph everything in one session with a template.
- Platform fit and fees (local marketplace, auction site, clothing resale apps, handmade/vintage
  sites, clean-out kits, yard sale): `references/selling-platforms.md`. Fees change; check each
  platform before quoting.
- Safety: meet local buyers in public (police-station lots are common; confirm locally), no checks,
  no overpayment schemes, release items only after payment clears.
- Tax [IRS 1099-K FAQ; Topic 506]: marketplaces report sales to the IRS above a threshold that has
  changed repeatedly; check the IRS 1099-K FAQ for the current one (a state may be lower); payments are taxable unless the law says otherwise; subtract
  fees and shipping; selling a personal item for less than you paid is not taxable income (IRS
  search summary). Keep purchase records for anything sold at a gain.
- Consignment/buy-sell stores pay cash or credit, reject much, and want seasonal timing; check their website or ask by email or online form first.

### 3. Donating: what charities typically take
- **Large thrift charities usually accept:** books and media, clothing, shoes and linens,
  housewares, decor, lamps, furniture in good condition, small appliances that work, computers,
  toys and games (complete), sporting goods, bikes, tools, garden equipment, pet supplies.
- **Usually refused:** televisions (often), gas cans or gas-powered items with fuel, broken,
  stained or worn furniture, paint and chemicals, mattresses and bed components, construction and
  plumbing supplies, auto parts and tires, water heaters and furnaces, damaged appliances,
  recalled or banned items, anything with an odor.
- **Condition rules:** wash clothes, pair shoes, wrap breakables, include all pieces, test
  electrical items, wipe drives, never leave items outside unattended.
- **Building-material reuse stores** (e.g. Habitat ReStores) take furniture, cabinets, doors,
  windows, fixtures, lumber, tools and working appliances, often with a per-appliance fee and
  free curbside pickup in their area; they refuse mattresses, gas appliances, ripped or stained
  items and partial flooring. Rules vary by store; read the current acceptance list.
- **Specialty outlets:** medical-equipment lending closets, children's consignment, retailer
  electronics take-back.
- The charity does not value your donation; you list and value items yourself and ask a tax
  professional.

### 4. Hazardous materials [EPA HHW]
Original container, tightly closed, labeled, never mixed; never down a drain, toilet or storm
sewer. County household-hazardous-waste facilities usually take paint, flammables, cleaners,
aerosols, fluorescent bulbs, batteries, lead-acid batteries, small propane tanks, pesticides and
solvents, free for residents; many run a free reuse shelf (check it before buying paint or lawn
chemicals). Larger propane tanks go back to a propane supplier. Lithium-ion batteries: bag each,
tape terminals, never trash or curbside, do not pile [EPA; NFPA]; e-bike batteries via bike shops
or a battery-recycling locator. Paint: paint-stewardship programs (e.g. PaintCare in participating
states) take house paint, stains and clear coatings free at drop-off sites; dried latex is often
allowed in the trash (verify locally).

### 5. E-waste
County programs typically take computers, monitors, phones, TVs, printers, networking gear,
cables, game systems and small electronics, often for a per-pound fee, and typically refuse small
kitchen appliances, hygiene devices, medical devices, toys and smoke/CO alarms. Retailers run
take-back for some items. Before disposal: wipe drives (factory reset, remove SIM and SD cards),
remove batteries.

### 6. Mattresses, appliances, tires, furniture
Mattresses: charities refuse them; county or nonprofit mattress recyclers usually charge a flat
fee per piece and reject wet, moldy or bed-bug items; some retailers haul away on delivery.
Appliances: transfer stations charge per unit, more with refrigerant; scrap-metal yards take
metal. Tires: per-tire fees, sometimes a per-trip cap by state law. Furniture: donate first,
otherwise charged as regular waste.

### 7. Medicines [FDA]
Best: take-back (pharmacy kiosks, DEA collectors, mail-back envelopes). If none: mix with used
coffee grounds or cat litter, seal, trash, scratch out personal info; flush only items on FDA's
flush list.

### 8. Trash and transfer stations
Check the site's status page or official listing the day of a haul: sites close for wind and holidays, and uncovered or unsecured
loads are often charged double. Curbside recycling is usually loose, not bagged; plastic bags,
shredded paper and metal hangers usually go to drop-off.

### 9. Building the local directory (for the household's own skill)
For each destination record: name, address, phone, hours as found, what it accepts and refuses,
fees, the source URL, and the date checked. Mark anything not confirmed on an official page as
**UNCONFIRMED**. Re-check before sending anyone, because fees and sites change.

### 10. Agent checklist per item
1. Identify category and hazard.
2. Check condition and completeness against section 3.
3. Decide sell vs donate with the ~$25 rule and platform fees.
4. Pick a destination from the household's directory and confirm hours on the day.
5. Pack: bag clothes, box housewares, tape battery terminals, cover loads.
6. Photograph the load or keep the receipt for taxes; file it (`household-paper-flow`).

## Pitfalls
- Donating incomplete, stained, broken or recalled items.
- Sending someone to a site from an old directory without confirming hours that day.
- Listing low-value items online; letting a "to sell" pile become storage.
- Quoting platform fees or disposal fees from memory.
- Putting lithium batteries, paint or chemicals in trash or curbside bins.

## Verification
- Every outbound item has a destination and a date it leaves the house.
- Hazardous items went only to a hazardous-waste route.
- Directory entries used today were checked recently or marked UNCONFIRMED and confirmed on the
  official page, by email or by online form.

## Household specifics
This skill is the method only. The agent's own local skill holds the household's local directory:
named charities, recycling centers, hazardous-waste and e-waste sites, landfill or transfer
station, hours, fees and phone numbers. It wins over the typical rules here.
