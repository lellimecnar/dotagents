# Dialogue and subtext

## The iceberg (Hemingway, Death in the Afternoon, 1932, ch. 16)
"If a writer of prose knows enough about what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the
reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the
writer had stated them. The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above
water. A writer who omits things because he does not know them only makes hollow places in his writing."

Rule: omit from knowledge, never from ignorance. Before cutting a stated feeling, know exactly what it is,
then give the reader the surface that implies it.

## Explaining dialogue (Browne & King, Self-Editing for Fiction Writers, "Dialogue Mechanics")
- "'You can't be serious,' she said in astonishment": the line already conveys astonishment, so the
  explanation patronizes. If the line does *not* convey it, the explanation won't fix it. Rewrite the line.
- Also watch for narration that explains the *content* of a speech before or after it (the Percy and
  wombats example).

## Workshop terms (Turkey City Lexicon, ed. Lewis Shiner and Bruce Sterling, SFWA)
- **Countersinking:** "expositional redundancy in which the action clearly implied in dialogue is made
  explicit." Example: "'Let's get out of here,' he said, urging her to leave."
- **"As You Know Bob":** characters tell each other things they already know, for the reader.
- **Brenda Starr dialogue:** long talk with no physical setting. Anchor speech with action beats.
- **Said-bookism / Tom Swifty:** use `said`; drop adverb props.
- **Laughtrack:** characters laugh at their own jokes or cry loudly so the reader is told how to feel.

## Tests
1. Read each speech, then the sentence after it. If that sentence restates the speech's intent or emotion,
   cut it.
2. For each emotional exchange, ask what the most important unsaid thing is. If someone says it, try
   moving it under the surface.
3. Could the line come from another character? If yes, revoice it against the voice profile.
4. Check each line against the speaker's voice profile (register, contractions, verbal tics). Where the
   profile and the manuscript disagree, flag it as a canon question; do not silently fix either side.

## Subtext moves
- Talk about the task, mean the relationship: practical talk (fuel, repairs, a meal) can carry fear or love.
  Let the task do the feeling and do not gloss it.
- A gesture that interrupts a speech works only if left unexplained.
- Withheld truth: a character visibly swallowing a sentence keeps the reader in on it and the listener out.
- Humour as deflection belongs right after a disaster or decision.
- For a restrained character, consider whether gesture, silence or action serves the moment better than explicit dialogue. Follow any established voice rule.
