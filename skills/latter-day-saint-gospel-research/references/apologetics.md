# Apologetics and difficult questions

Goal: the asker leaves with an accurate picture of the evidence, the Church's own account, faithful answers, and
what remains open. Credibility comes from honesty; one overclaim discredits the rest.

## Method
1. **State the claim fairly**, in the critic's own words from their source (or the asker's words), at its
   strongest. Do not answer a weaker version.
2. **Separate the parts**: historical fact claims, interpretation, and the conclusion drawn. Each gets its own
   evidence.
3. **Start with the Church's own treatment**: Gospel Topics Essays, Church History Topics, Saints, the Joseph Smith
   Papers, official statements. They often acknowledge the difficult facts directly; cite them so the asker sees the
   Church is not hiding them.
4. **Primary sources next**: the original documents the claim rests on (Joseph Smith Papers, contemporary accounts,
   manuscripts). Check whether the claim matches what they actually say.
5. **Faithful scholarship** (FAIR, Interpreter, BYU Studies, Maxwell Institute, Scripture Central, RSC): give the
   strongest faithful responses, attributed to their authors, and note where faithful scholars disagree.
6. **Say what evidence cannot settle.** Archaeology, DNA, linguistics and history can inform but not prove or
   disprove revelation. Do not claim proof either way; say what the evidence supports and how strongly.
7. **Testimony is labelled as testimony**, offered where the asker wants it, never as a substitute for evidence on
   a factual question.

## Rules
- Represent critics accurately; no straw men, no motive-guessing, no mockery.
- Never minimize documented history (e.g. what the Gospel Topics Essays acknowledge) and never inflate it.
- When sources conflict, show the conflict with dates and sources.
- When the honest answer is "we don't know", say it, and give what the Church and scholars have said about the
  open question.
- Recommend the asker read the primary and Church sources themselves, with links.

## Politically charged applications
- Date historical statements and distinguish their audience and context from current published teaching. Do not present a historical statement as current teaching without verifying it.
- Help people recognize these things by scriptural traits (power and gain, flattery and fair promises, grievance
  turned into hatred, freedom for themselves only, sorting people into tribes) rather than by labeling groups.
  BYU scholarship (Religious Educator 25:1, 2024) warns against labeling "without proper authority".
- Keep claims about effect apart from claims about motive. Many followers of a harmful movement sincerely believe its
  promise (Hel. 6:38, "seduced the more part of the righteous"). Motive claims need their own evidence.
- For contested terms, state how relevant sources define the term. Give the main criticism and
  the strongest argument from the other side, each in its own words, then suggest wording that keeps the point.
- When statistics come in, check the actual poll and note when the numbers depend on how the question was worded.
  Keep it to one line when the number was only an illustration (see sacrament-talk-prep, "Study depth vs. pulpit").
