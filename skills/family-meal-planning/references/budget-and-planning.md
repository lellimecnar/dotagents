# Budget and planning reference

## Budget benchmark: USDA Food Plans
Source: USDA Food Plans, Monthly Cost of Food Reports, https://www.fna.usda.gov/research/cnpp/usda-food-plans/cost-food-monthly-reports
- Four plans: Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, Liberal. All assume every meal and snack is prepared at home (monthly report).
- Costs are per person by age and sex, for people in 4-person households. To get a household total: (1) add each person's cost, then (2) adjust for size: 1 person +20%, 2 +10%, 3 +5%, 4 none, **5 or 6 people −5%**, 7 or more −10% (monthly report footnote).
- Monthly = weekly x 4.333 (monthly report).
- Current costs: download the latest monthly Cost of Food report from the source page above before
  quoting; read each person's weekly cost by age and sex, apply the method above, and state the
  report month used.

- Use: "tight budget" means planning at or below the household's Thrifty total; a plan that needs more should say so and say why.

## Unit pricing
Source: NIST SP 1181, Unit Pricing Guide (2025 edition), https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/1181e2025/NIST.SP.1181e2025.html
- Unit price = cost per unit of measure (per oz, lb, fl oz, count); NIST calls it one of the most effective tools for comparing value across pack sizes, including when packages are downsized ("shrinkflation").
- Retail labels are not always uniform (some categories mix units), so convert to one unit before comparing.
- Multi-buy deals ("2 for $5") and loyalty or digital-coupon prices each have their own unit price; compare the one you will actually pay.
- "Servings" or "loads" are not legal units for unit pricing; compare by weight, volume or count.
- My rules: compare the same unit across brands and sizes; the bigger pack wins only if it will be used before spoiling; check online cart unit prices against the shelf tag when building a cart.

## Planning patterns (practitioner methods; safety limits from FSIS)
- **Theme nights:** a fixed category per weeknight (soup, taco, pasta, pizza, breakfast-for-dinner) reduces decisions and makes rotation easy.
- **Cook once, eat twice (planned-overs):** cook a larger protein early in the week (roast, slow-cooker pork or chicken, beans) and turn it into a different meal within 3–4 days (tacos, fried rice, soup, sandwiches). Leftover limit: refrigerator 3–4 days (FSIS, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety).
- **Batch cooking:** cook grains, beans and a sauce base once for several meals; cool in shallow containers and refrigerate within 2 hours (FSIS, same page).
- **Freezer meals:** double a freezer-friendly dish; freeze in meal-size flat portions; label name and date; use within about 3 months for quality (FSIS says 3–4 months for leftovers). Hold back pasta, rice and potatoes (Serious Eats, https://seriouseats.com/12-freezer-friendly-soups-11910541). Reheat frozen leftovers without thawing if needed, to 165 °F (FSIS).
- **Slow-cooker or start-early nights** for evenings when the cook is out until dinner: load thawed ingredients from the fridge, low all day (FSIS slow-cooker rules in `food-safety.md`).
- **Leftover night** once a week to use what is in the fridge before day 4.
- Plan shopping in waves: non-perishables first, sturdy produce next, delicate greens and herbs last (Serious Eats meal-prep timeline, https://seriouseats.com/thanksgiving-timeline-meal-prep-guide-12207008).

## Budget levers, in order of effect (my method)
1. Use what is already in the pantry, fridge and freezer first.
2. Cheaper protein mix: beans, lentils, eggs, whole chickens and leg quarters, pork shoulder, ground meat stretched with beans or vegetables. Slow cookers suit cheaper, tougher cuts (MSU Extension, https://extension.msstate.edu/news/feature-story/2022/use-slow-cookers-properly-avoid-food-borne-illness). DGA lists beans, peas and lentils as protein foods (https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf).
3. Frozen and canned vegetables and fruit with little or no added sugar count (DGA).
4. Store brands and unit-price comparison.
5. Plan cross-use so nothing is bought for a single recipe (half a bunch of cilantro in two meals).
6. Cook from scratch for high-markup items (sauces, seasoning mixes, pizza dough) when time allows.

## Grocery-list method
1. List each recipe's ingredients with quantities scaled to the household (and any extra protein for a targets brief).
2. Subtract what is on hand.
3. Merge duplicates, round to package sizes, note substitutes that are acceptable.
4. Group by store section.
5. If authorized, update the designated shopping list and respect its convention for completed or recurring items.
6. Estimate the total from current store prices when building a cart; never place the order.
