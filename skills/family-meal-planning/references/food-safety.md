# Food safety reference (home kitchen)

Primary sources: USDA FSIS, FoodSafety.gov (USDA/FDA/CDC), FDA Food Code, FDA allergen labeling, FARE. Every number below carries its source.

## Safe minimum internal temperatures (measure with a food thermometer)
Source: FSIS Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart

| Food | Minimum internal temperature |
|---|---|
| Beef, pork, veal, lamb steaks, chops, roasts | 145 °F, then rest at least 3 minutes |
| Ground meats (beef, pork, veal, lamb) | 160 °F |
| All poultry, including ground poultry, giblets, stuffing | 165 °F |
| Fresh or smoked ham (uncooked) | 145 °F + 3-minute rest |
| Fully cooked ham, reheating | 140 °F if packaged in a USDA-inspected plant; otherwise 165 °F |
| Eggs (egg dishes) | 160 °F |
| Fish and shellfish | 145 °F |
| Leftovers and casseroles | 165 °F |

When roasting meat and poultry, use an oven temperature no lower than 325 °F (FSIS Danger Zone page, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f).

## The danger zone and the 2-hour rule
Source: FSIS Danger Zone page (above) and FSIS Leftovers and Food Safety, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety
- Bacteria grow most rapidly between 40 °F and 140 °F, doubling in as little as 20 minutes.
- Never leave perishable food out more than 2 hours; 1 hour above 90 °F.
- Hold hot food at 140 °F or above; cold food at 40 °F or below.
- Reheat to 165 °F or until hot and steaming; bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil; cover and rotate in the microwave and check several spots.

**Thresholds differ between consumer and retail guidance.** FSIS uses 40–140 °F for home cooks; the FDA Food Code (retail) uses 41–135 °F (FDA, https://www.fda.gov/media/181882/download). For home cooking follow FSIS (the stricter numbers at both ends).

## Cooling cooked food
- FSIS (home): divide large amounts into shallow containers; cut roasts into smaller parts; hot food may go straight into the fridge or into an ice or cold-water bath first; refrigerate within 2 hours (FSIS Leftovers page, above).
- FDA Food Code (retail benchmark, useful to judge whether a method is fast enough): cool from 135 °F to 70 °F within 2 hours, then to 41 °F or less within the next 4 hours; methods include shallow pans, smaller or thinner portions, stirring in an ice-water bath, adding ice as an ingredient, and leaving loosely covered while cooling (FDA, https://www.fda.gov/media/181882/download). The Oregon Food Code fact sheet adds a 2-inch maximum depth for shallow-layer cooling (https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/HEALTHYENVIRONMENTS/FOODSAFETY/Documents/FactSheet31Cooling.pdf).
- FDA says loosely covered or uncovered containers are to be placed in cooling or cold-holding equipment, and uncovered food must be protected from overhead contamination; Oregon says shallow-layer food goes into refrigeration at 41 °F or lower (FDA and Oregon sheets above).
- Rule I follow: divide big batches promptly into containers about 2 inches deep or less and put them into the refrigerator immediately (or first into an ice-water bath or another rapid-cooling method, then the refrigerator). Never leave them on the counter to cool. In the refrigerator leave them loosely covered, or uncovered only if protected from contamination (nothing stored above that can drip, no raw food overhead); put the lid on once the food is cold.

## Leftovers and storage times
- Leftovers: refrigerator 3–4 days; freezer 3–4 months for best quality, safe indefinitely when frozen (FSIS Leftovers page).
- Cold Food Storage Chart (FoodSafety.gov, https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts): fridge at 40 °F or below, freezer at 0 °F or below; freezer times are for quality only.

| Item | Refrigerator | Freezer (quality) |
|---|---|---|
| Egg, chicken, ham, tuna, macaroni salads | 3–4 days | does not freeze well |
| Hot dogs, opened / unopened | 1 week / 2 weeks | 1–2 months |
| Lunch meat, opened or deli-sliced | 3–5 days | 1–2 months |
| Fresh beef, veal, lamb, pork steaks, chops, roasts | 3–5 days | 4–12 months |
| Fresh poultry, whole | 1–2 days | 1 year |
| Fresh poultry, pieces | 1–2 days | 9 months |
| Fatty fish (salmon, tuna, catfish) | 1–3 days | 2–3 months |
| Lean fish (cod, flounder, haddock) | 1–3 days | 6–8 months |
| Shrimp | 3–5 days | 6–18 months |
| Raw eggs in shell | 3–5 weeks | do not freeze in shell |
| Hard-cooked eggs | 1 week | do not freeze |
| Soups and stews with meat or vegetables | 3–4 days | 2–3 months |
| Cooked meat or poultry leftovers | 3–4 days | 2–6 months |
| Pizza | 3–4 days | 1–2 months |

**Sources disagree on cooked-meat freezer time** (FSIS Leftovers page: 3–4 months; FoodSafety.gov chart: 2–6 months). Both say this is quality only; I label freezer meals to use within 3 months.

For any item not listed, look it up in the USDA FoodKeeper (650+ foods): https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/foodkeeper-app

## Thawing
Source: FSIS The Big Thaw, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/big-thaw-safe-defrosting-methods
- Three safe ways: refrigerator, cold water, microwave. Cooking from frozen is safe and takes about 50% longer.
- Never thaw on the counter, in hot water, or in a garage, car, dishwasher or outdoors.
- Refrigerator: about 24 hours per 5 lb for large items; even 1 lb of ground meat or boneless chicken needs a full day. After fridge thawing, ground meat, stew meat, poultry and seafood keep 1–2 more days; red-meat roasts, chops and steaks 3–5 days. Fridge-thawed food may be refrozen uncooked (some quality loss).
- Cold water: leak-proof bag, submerge in cold tap water, change the water every 30 minutes; about 1 lb in an hour or less, 3–4 lb in 2–3 hours; cook immediately; cook before refreezing.
- Microwave: cook immediately after thawing.
- Leftovers thawed in the fridge: use within 3–4 days or refreeze; a portion can be removed and the rest refrozen without reheating (FSIS Leftovers page).

## Freezing
- Food kept at 0 °F or below stays safe; freezer burn (grey-brown leathery spots) is a quality problem, not a safety problem; cut it away (NDSU Extension, https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/questions-and-answers-about-storing-food-freezer).
- Starches in soups (noodles, rice, potatoes) can turn mushy after freezing; blended soups and stocks freeze well (Serious Eats, https://seriouseats.com/12-freezer-friendly-soups-11910541). Rule: freeze the base, add the starch when reheating.

## Slow cookers
Sources: FSIS Slow Cookers and Food Safety, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/slow-cookers-and-food-safety ; CSU Extension, https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/crockpot-and-slow-cooker-food-safety
- Always thaw meat or poultry first; frozen pieces may not reach 140 °F fast enough (FSIS, CSU).
- Keep cut-up meat and vegetables refrigerated separately until loading; put vegetables in first; keep the lid on (FSIS).
- If possible, start on high for the first hour; low the whole time is also safe (FSIS).
- Fill at least half and no more than two-thirds full (CSU).
- Power cut while nobody is home: throw the food away even if it looks done (FSIS).
- Do not reheat leftovers in a slow cooker; reheat to 165 °F on the stove, microwave or oven, then hold in a preheated slow cooker at 140 °F or above (FSIS).
- High altitude: start on high for the first hour; if adjustable, keep food at 200 °F or higher; allow longer times and do not lift the lid (CSU High Elevation guide, https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/high-elevation-food-preparation-guide).

## High altitude
Source: FSIS High Altitude Cooking, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/high-altitude-cooking ; CSU Extension (above)
- High altitude usually means 3,000 ft or more; water boils just under 1 °F lower for every 500 ft (about 203 °F at 5,000 ft and 198 °F at 7,500 ft).
- Boiled, simmered and braised foods take longer; turning up the heat does not help. Braises may need about a quarter more time at 5,000 ft (CSU). Oven temperatures are unaffected (CSU).
- Lower deep-fry oil temperature about 3 °F per 1,000 ft (FSIS, CSU).
- Safe internal temperatures do not change; always check with a thermometer.

## Allergens
- The 9 US major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame. Sesame became the ninth under the FASTER Act; labeling has been required since January 1, 2023 (FDA, https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen).
- The allergen source must appear in the ingredient list (for example "flour (wheat)") or in a "Contains" statement (FDA, https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/have-food-allergies-read-label). Products without updated sesame labeling may still be on shelves; read every label every time.
- Cross-contact (FARE, https://foodallergy.org/resources/how-avoid-cross-contact-pdf): cooking does not remove allergens; a mistake cannot be fixed by picking the allergen off, so start over. Wash utensils, boards and pans with soap and water; use separate (colour-coded) tools; cook the allergy-safe food first and keep it covered; hand sanitizer or water alone does not remove allergens, so wash hands with soap and water.

## Kitchen basics (FSIS Safe Temperature Chart page)
Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill. Wash hands with warm water and soap for at least 20 seconds.
