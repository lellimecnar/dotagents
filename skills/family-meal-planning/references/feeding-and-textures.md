# Feeding children, picky eating, sensory needs and textures

## Satter Division of Responsibility in Feeding (sDOR)
Source: Ellyn Satter Institute, https://www.ellynsatterinstitute.org/how-to-feed/the-division-of-responsibility-in-feeding/
- Toddlers through adolescents: the parent is responsible for **what, when and where**; the child for **how much and whether**.
- Parent's jobs: choose and prepare the food; provide regular meals and snacks; make eating times pleasant; show by example how to behave at family meals; be considerate of the child's lack of food experience **without catering to likes and dislikes**; no food or drinks except water between meal and snack times; let the child grow into the body that is right for them.
- Trust the child to eat, eat the amount they need, learn to eat the food the family enjoys, and grow predictably.
- Satter advises against putting children on weight-loss diets or restricting them; dieting tends to backfire (same source).

## AAP guidance on picky eating
- Picky eating is common, especially between ages 2 and 4, and usually short-lived; eat family meals together and model adventurous eating; keep regular, structured meal and snack times without devices; let the child choose what and how much from what is offered; **do not make separate meals**; include at least one food the child likes; allow refusal but offer it again later; it can take 15–20 tries to like a new food; involve kids in selecting, growing and cooking food (AAP HealthyChildren.org, https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/How-Do-I-Help-My-Picky-Eater-Try-More-Foods.aspx).
- It can take 13 or more exposures; "trying" includes having it on the plate, touching, smelling, licking, or chewing and politely spitting out; offer one familiar plus one new food; praise curiosity, not the amount eaten; limit choices to two options (AAP, https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/nutrition/Pages/taste-touch-explore-make-nutritious-food-fun-for-young-eaters.aspx).
- **Exposure counts disagree:** DGA 2025–2030 says 8–10 (https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf); AAP says 13 or more, up to 15–20. I plan for 10–15 exposures, so I keep re-offering past the DGA number.

## Practical patterns (my method, built on the sources above)
- Deconstruct: serve components separately (taco bar, rice-bowl bar, baked-potato bar, pasta with sauce on the side).
- Every meal contains a safe food each child usually eats (bread, rice, fruit, plain pasta).
- Introduce change one feature at a time: same food in a new shape, then a new sauce, then a new texture.
- Small "learning portions" (one or two bites' worth) of the new food to cut waste.
- Children help: pick a new vegetable at the store, wash, stir, assemble.

## IDDSI framework (dysphagia diets)
Source: IDDSI, https://iddsi.org/framework ; level handouts at https://iddsi.org (licensed CC BY-SA 4.0; derivative works beyond translation not permitted, so never alter the levels).
- 8 levels, 0–7: drinks are measured at levels 0–4, foods at levels 3–7. The clinician recommends the level after an assessment. Test foods under serving conditions, especially temperature (IDDSI).
- IDDSI warns that altering the framework has been associated with adverse events including choking and death (IDDSI).

| Food level | Description | Key test (IDDSI / NHS summaries) |
|---|---|---|
| 3 Liquidised | (also a drink level) eaten with a spoon, no chewing | follow the IDDSI flow and fork-drip tests for the prescribed product |
| 4 Pureed | no lumps, not sticky, liquid does not separate; holds shape; no chewing | spoon tilt: holds shape and the whole spoonful slides off easily; sits in a mound on a fork |
| 5 Minced & moist | soft, moist, no separate thin liquid; small lumps easy to squash with the tongue; lumps no bigger than 4 mm adults, 2 mm children | particles pass between fork prongs and mash with little pressure |
| 6 Soft & bite-sized | soft, tender, moist; chewing required; no knife needed; pieces no bigger than 1.5 x 1.5 cm adults, 8 mm children | fork pressure: press until the thumbnail blanches white; food squashes and does not return to shape |
| 7 Easy to chew / Regular | everyday foods; level 7 Easy to Chew is soft and tender textures only | as prescribed |

Sources for the level details: St George's University Hospitals NHS, https://stgeorges.nhs.uk/service/therapies/speech-and-language-therapy/our-new-fluid-and-diet-terminology/iddsi-diet-levels-information ; IDDSI Level 6 adult handout (no regular dry bread at level 6; meat that cannot be served soft and tender should be served as Minced & Moist), https://iddsi.org/images/Publications-Resources/PatientHandouts/English/Adults/6_soft_bite_sized_adult_consumer_handout_30jan2019.pdf ; IDDSI food poster, https://iddsi.org/images/Publications-Resources/Poster/OtherPosters/iddsi_framework_poster_a3_a_p_food_only_final_with_bleed_jan2020.pdf

Verify the current official IDDSI definition and tests for the prescribed level, and follow the person’s clinical instructions.

**Family-meal adaptation for a texture level:** cook the family base meal, then take that person's portion before final crisping or crunchy toppings and modify it (braise until fork-tender, then chop to the size limit, mince or puree with sauce so no thin liquid separates). Test every batch. Moist braises, stews, chili without skins and chunks, egg dishes, and pasta in sauce adapt well; dry bread, crunchy, stringy, fibrous, and mixed thin-liquid-plus-solid foods do not.

## Choking hazards for young children
- AAP: keep high-risk foods from children until age 4 or older depending on development; round, firm foods such as hot dogs or grapes can be served if chopped into tiny pieces. The list: hot dogs; hard, gooey or sticky candy; chewing gum; nuts and seeds; whole grapes; raw vegetables such as carrot sticks; raw fruit chunks such as apple pieces; popcorn; thick chunks of nut butter (spread thinly); marshmallows; meat sticks or sausages; chunks of meat; chunks of cheese or string cheese (https://healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Choking-Prevention.aspx).
- CDC (from USDA WIC) adds: whole corn kernels, uncut cherry tomatoes, whole canned fruit pieces, uncut berries, cherries or melon balls, raisins, whole beans, bones in meat or fish, chips, pretzels, crackers or breads with seeds or whole kernels (https://cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/foods-and-drinks/choking-hazards.html).

## Red flags: refer, do not manage at home
- ASHA signs of feeding and swallowing disorders: arching or stiffening, trouble breathing while eating, refusing to eat or drink, eating only certain textures, taking a long time to eat, pocketing food, problems chewing, coughing or gagging during meals, drooling or liquid from the nose, a gurgly, hoarse or breathy voice during or after meals, frequent vomiting, poor weight gain or growth. Next step: talk to the child's doctor and see a speech-language pathologist trained in feeding and swallowing (https://asha.org/public/speech/swallowing/Feeding-and-Swallowing-Disorders-in-Children).
- ASHA pediatric feeding disorder signs include meals or snacks taking longer than 30 minutes and refusing foods of certain textures, brands or colours (https://asha.org/news/2023/asha-encourages-families-to-learn-the-signs-of-pediatric-feeding-disorder).
- ARFID signs (NEDA): dramatic restriction in types or amount of food, only certain textures, fear of choking or vomiting, lack of interest in food, a range of preferred foods that narrows over time, weight loss or faltering growth; treatment is by a multidisciplinary team, and early help matters (https://www.nationaleatingdisorders.org/avoidant-restrictive-food-intake-disorder-arfid/).
