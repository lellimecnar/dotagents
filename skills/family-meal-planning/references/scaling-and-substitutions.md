# Recipe scaling and substitutions

## Scaling cooked dishes (America's Test Kitchen)
Source: ATK, Tips for Doubling Your Favorite Recipes, https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/1921-tips-for-doubling-your-favorite-recipes-and-dinner-staples
- Do not overcrowd: double chili in a stockpot or two Dutch ovens; sear meat in batches; a doubled 8-inch square recipe goes in a 13 x 9-inch pan.
- Pressure cooker: doubling needs double the pot size (4 qt to 8 qt); with a 6-qt pot, increase by half; cooking time stays the same.
- Judge doneness by temperature and the recipe's other cues, not the clock.
- Two dishes in one oven: leave space between pans and walls, stagger them, rotate and switch halfway.
- Simple recipes (salads, dressings, sauces, pasta, roast salmon) can be multiplied straight; still check that the cooking vessel fits.
- Salt and strong spices: a practitioner source suggests about 1.5x when doubling, then taste and adjust, because seasoning is perceived rather than a fixed ratio (CookToolbox, citing ATK, https://cooktoolbox.com/blog/why-doubling-a-recipe-fails). This is a reputable-practitioner rule, not test-kitchen data; I use it because under-seasoning can be fixed and over-seasoning cannot.

## Scaling baked goods to a different pan (ATK)
Source: ATK, How to Adjust Cake Recipes for Different Size Pans, https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/8002-cake-pan-sizes
- Factor = area of the new pan / area of the original pan (same height). A 9-inch round is more than 25% larger than an 8-inch round.

| From \ To | 8-in round | 9-in round | 8x8 | 9x9 | 13x9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8-in round | 1.0 | 1.3 | 1.3 | 1.6 | 2.3 |
| 9-in round | 0.8 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.3 | 1.8 |
| 8x8 square | 0.8 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.3 | 1.8 |
| 9x9 square | 0.6 | 0.8 | 0.8 | 1.0 | 1.4 |
| 13x9 | 0.4 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.7 | 1.0 |

- Round awkward fractions (2.37 tsp to 2.5 tsp); a large egg is about 4 Tbsp beaten, so half an egg is 2 Tbsp; check that the mixer can handle the volume; do not rely on the original bake times (ATK).

## Ingredient substitutions
Primary source: Colorado State University Extension, https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/ingredient-substitutions ; cross-check: UC ANR, https://ucanr.edu/media/234961 ; Utah State University Extension, https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1016&context=extension_histfood

| Need | Substitute |
|---|---|
| 1 tsp double-acting baking powder | ¼ tsp baking soda + ½ tsp cream of tartar + ¼ tsp cornstarch; or ¼ tsp baking soda + ½ cup buttermilk or yogurt (cut recipe liquid by ½ cup) (CSU) |
| 1 cup buttermilk | 1 Tbsp vinegar or lemon juice + milk to make 1 cup, stand 5 minutes (UC ANR, USU); or 1 cup plain yogurt (USU) |
| 1 large egg (baking) | ¼ cup commercial egg substitute; reconstituted powdered egg; in baking, 1 of every 3 eggs can be replaced with 1 Tbsp cornstarch (CSU); 2 Tbsp mayonnaise in cake batter (UC ANR) |
| 1 cup self-rising flour | **sources differ:** 1 cup all-purpose + 1¼ tsp baking powder + ¼ tsp salt (CSU) vs 1 cup all-purpose (minus 2 tsp) + 1½ tsp baking powder + ½ tsp salt (UC ANR, USU). I use the 1½ tsp / ½ tsp version because two independent extension sources agree. |
| 1 cup cake flour | 1 cup minus 2 Tbsp all-purpose flour (UC ANR) |
| 1 cup all-purpose flour (more whole grain) | ½ cup whole wheat + ½ cup all-purpose (UC ANR) |
| 1 cup yogurt | 1 cup buttermilk; 1 cup cottage cheese blended smooth; 1 cup sour cream (CSU) |
| 1 tsp allspice | ½ tsp cinnamon + ½ tsp ground cloves (CSU) |

**Allergen substitutions** follow the allergy, not convenience: confirm every substitute's label is free of the allergen (FDA labeling rules in `food-safety.md`). Mayonnaise contains egg, so it is not an egg-allergy swap.

## High altitude adjustments
See `food-safety.md` (FSIS and CSU Extension): longer simmer and braise times (about a quarter more at 5,000 ft), lower deep-fry temperature about 3 °F per 1,000 ft, oven temperatures unchanged for roasting, slow cookers started on high. For baked goods at altitude, extra-large eggs add structure (FSIS High Altitude Cooking, https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/high-altitude-cooking); use a tested high-altitude recipe when one exists.
