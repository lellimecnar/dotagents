# Family meal planning and home cooking

The method for any meal-planning, grocery, recipe or food-safety task for a household (any size, any budget, including members with special feeding needs). Numbers, tables and source URLs live in `references/`; this file is the procedure and the decision rules. Keep household facts out of this skill. Use only planning details the requester supplies or authorizes you to access.

## When to Use
Any task that plans meals, writes or changes a grocery list, adapts or scales a recipe, answers a food-safety or storage question, feeds a picky eater or someone on a texture-modified diet, turns authorized nutrition goals into meals, or [imports recipes into a recipe manager](references/recipe-import.md).

## 1. Gather inputs before planning (in this order)
1. **Hard constraints first.** Allergies (the major allergens on the food regulator's current list, checked at task time, and any others), prescribed textures (an IDDSI level set by a speech-language pathologist), medical diets. Confirm them with the person, an authorized caregiver or the documented care plan; never infer them. If one is unclear, ask before planning.
2. **Existing systems.** The household's own meal service or plan, recorded preferences and requests, the current shopping list (do not re-add checked items), what is already in the fridge (with cook dates) and freezer. Work with the existing system and fill its gaps; do not replace it.
3. **The week's shape.** Which nights are late, short or away; who cooks; when someone can start a dish early (slow cooker, prep-ahead); any other scheduling or availability constraints.
4. **Budget.** Benchmark with the USDA Food Plans (Thrifty Food Plan per person by age and sex, with the household-size adjustment). See `references/budget-and-planning.md`.
5. **Targets.** If provided, use the person’s authorized nutrition goals or care plan (section 3).

## 2. Build the week (step by step)
1. **Skeleton.** Mark fixed nights first (meal-service picks, standing traditions), then busy nights (slow cooker, sheet pan, planned-overs, freezer meal), then give the remaining nights a theme (taco night, soup night, pizza night) to cut decisions.
2. **Anchor proteins.** Choose 2–3 economical proteins that cross-use (cook once, eat twice). Planned-overs must be eaten within 3–4 days of cooking or frozen promptly.
3. **One freezer batch** when time allows: double a freezer-friendly dish (chili, sauce, blended soup; keep pasta, rice and potatoes out of the frozen portion). Label with name and date.
4. **One base meal, served deconstructed.** Sauce on the side, a plain starch, raw and cooked vegetable options, and at least one food each child usually accepts. Everyone eats the same meal; nobody gets a separate order.
5. **Check nutrition across the week, not per plate.** Open the dietary guidance for the household's jurisdiction; record the edition and check date. Use the relevant age groups, food-group guidance and limits from that source, subject to prescribed diets. See `references/nutrition-targets.md` for a dated U.S. example.
6. **Fold in any targets brief** (section 3).
7. **Safety pass.** Thaw plan (fridge thawing takes about a day even for a pound of ground meat), slow-cooker rules (thaw first, never reheat in it), leftover windows, allergen cross-contact, texture levels, under-4 choking modifications, high-altitude timing. See `references/food-safety.md`.
8. **Grocery list.** Derive from the recipes, subtract pantry and leftovers, group by store section, compare **unit prices** (same unit across sizes; check sale and multi-buy unit prices), prefer store brands, seasonal and frozen produce, and bulk only for items that will be used before they spoil. Prepare carts and totals; never purchase.
9. **Deliver.** Few options, already prioritised, with the first physical action named ("Move the pork shoulder from the freezer to the fridge tonight"). Record plans only in authorized destinations and use the requester’s chosen process for calendar suggestions.

## 3. Folding a Nutrition targets brief into the family plan
**Rule: same base meal; adjust that person's portions and sides.** Cook a separate meal only when a clinician-prescribed diet makes the family meal unsafe for them.
1. **Protein portion.** Divide the person's daily protein target across their meals (use the brief's per-meal number if given). Size their portion from the food's own label or a food-composition database value for the form actually served (cooked, not raw), counting cooking fat. The "7 g protein per ounce" shortcut under-counts lean poultry; use it only for a rough family estimate, never for target work. Buy and cook enough extra protein in the family batch to cover the larger plate.
2. **Sides.** Energy-deficit target: extra non-starchy vegetables in place of a second starch serving. Higher energy target: an extra starch, dairy or healthy-fat side. The family dish stays the same.
3. **Table add-ons anyone can take:** plain Greek yogurt or cottage cheese, beans, eggs, milk, cheese.
4. **Children are not put on an adult's targets.** Children decide how much to eat from what is served (Satter Division of Responsibility); no restricting or pushing portions.
5. **Send it back instead of guessing** when the brief conflicts with an allergy, a texture prescription or a medical diet, when targets look extreme, or when it would need a separate meal most nights. Name the conflict to the trainer and, for medical issues, point to a registered dietitian.
6. **Whose number wins.** Protein guidance disagrees between the dietary guidelines, the RDA and sports-nutrition position stands (the reference lists one dated set of figures; check the current editions before quoting them). For the individual, follow the trainer's brief; for everyone else, use the current dietary guidelines' serving amounts.

## 4. Feeding children, picky eaters and texture needs
- **Division of Responsibility (Satter):** adults choose what, when and where; the child chooses whether and how much. No pressure, bribes, clean-plate rules or short-order cooking; regular meals and sit-down snacks.
- **Repeated exposure:** put a new or rejected food on the table many times in small amounts beside accepted foods. Sources differ (DGA: 8–10 exposures; AAP: 13 or more, up to 15–20); plan for 10–15 and do not count a refusal as failure.
- **Sensory and texture sensitivity:** serve components separately, keep one predictable safe food, change one feature at a time (shape, then sauce, then texture), and involve the child in shopping and prep.
- **Dysphagia (swallowing difficulty):** the texture level is a clinical prescription using the IDDSI framework (drinks 0–4, foods 3–7). Cook to the stated level and check it with the IDDSI tests (fork pressure, spoon tilt, particle size; children's sizes are smaller than adults'). Never choose, relax or "approximate" a level yourself, and never alter the framework.
- **Under-4 choking hazards:** cut round or firm foods (grapes, hot dogs, cherry tomatoes) small, spread nut butters thinly, and avoid nuts, popcorn and hard candy. See `references/feeding-and-textures.md`.

## 5. Stop and refer
- **Emergency now** (choking with trouble breathing, signs of a severe allergic reaction): tell the household to get emergency help immediately (911 in the US) and stop planning.
- **Pediatrician / feeding-trained speech-language pathologist:** coughing, gagging or a wet, gurgly voice at meals; pocketing food; meals regularly over 30 minutes; refusing whole texture groups; weight loss or poor growth.
- **Physician or eating-disorder team:** picky eating that keeps narrowing, fear of choking or vomiting driving avoidance, weight loss (possible ARFID).
- **Registered dietitian:** medical diets (diabetes, kidney disease, tube feeding, metabolic disorders), any weight-loss plan for a child, conflicting briefs.
- **Allergist:** any new or suspected reaction. Never "test" a food on someone.

## 6. Common mistakes
- Planning before reading the existing plan, the preference lists, the fridge and the shopping list.
- Planned-overs eaten after day 4; counter or hot-water thawing; frozen meat in a slow cooker; reheating leftovers in a slow cooker; cooling a big pot whole instead of in shallow containers.
- Judging doneness by colour or clock instead of a thermometer.
- Doubling everything, including salt, spices and pan, then cooking by the original time. Scale by pan area, cook in two vessels, judge by temperature, season to taste at the end.
- Comparing shelf prices instead of unit prices; buying bulk that spoils.
- Cooking separate meals for picky eaters, or forcing bites.
- Calling food "soft" when an IDDSI level was prescribed; guessing a level.
- Citing a guideline edition or a government food icon from memory. Confirm which edition and icon are current before naming them.
- Quoting a price, sale, food-plan cost or store stock from memory or an old cart instead of checking it today and giving the date.
- Target arithmetic that mixes raw and cooked database entries or leaves out cooking oil.
- Rounding a miss away: outside the brief's range is a miss; say so and why.
- Inferring a cooking yield from raw-versus-cooked nutrient ratios; use a published yield value or measured weights, else mark it unverified.
- Trusting a familiar product's label from memory. Read the allergen statement on every purchase.
- Handing a decision-maker a long, unsorted list.
