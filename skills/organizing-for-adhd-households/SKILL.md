# Organizing for ADHD and support-needs households

## Purpose
Help a household where an adult, teen or child has ADHD, sensory needs or a disability set up
organizing systems that survive inconsistency, low-energy days, shame and shifting family roles.
The expert method is: design for the worst day, put supports where the task happens, cut steps,
and adjust the system rather than the person.

You are an organizer, not a clinician. Never say a named person "has" or "probably has" ADHD,
autism or any condition. Work from what the family tells you and from what you observe in the
home. Ask "what happens at that moment?" not "what is wrong?".

## When to use
- A household member's ADHD, sensory profile or disability shapes how organizing must work.
- A system keeps dying after a good start, or a couple fights over chores and clutter.
- Deciding who owns what with teens and children, or setting up a low-energy-day protocol.
- Not for: sentimental objects, hoarding signs or family conflict over belongings (use
  `clutter-feelings-and-hoarding`); picking a decluttering method (use `decluttering-method-guide`).

## Method

## The 12 rules to apply first

1. **Design for the worst day, not the best.** A system that needs daily willpower will fail.
   Build three versions of every routine: ideal, good enough, and minimum (one step). [popular claim]
2. **Make it visible.** Open shelves, clear bins, hooks, trays, a see-through "act now" folder.
   Visible reminders are common in chronic disorganization. [professional practice]
3. **Put the support where the task happens** (timer in the kitchen, checklist on the door, bin
   by the spot where things are dropped). Barkley's framing: the problem is doing, not knowing, so
   supports go at the point of performance. [clinical]
4. **Fewer steps beats better labels.** Count steps from "done using it" to "put away". Target
   one or two. Replace lids, drawers and folders with hooks, baskets and trays.
5. **Reduce stuff before buying containers.** Less stuff means less to find and less to decide.
   [professional practice]
6. **Start small, start easy.** Begin with a small piece of real work (one drawer, one shelf); action
   tends to create motivation, not the other way round. [professional practice]
7. **"Good enough" is the standard.** Perfectionism stalls starting. Organizers use the motto
   "Done is better than perfect" (ICD fact sheet). [professional practice]
8. **Work alongside someone.** Body doubling: another person present while you work. Evidence is
   limited; many people like it, some are distracted by it. Test it, never promise it. [research: limited]
9. **Use interest, novelty, challenge and deadlines** as fuel (timers, races, music, swap tasks).
   Importance and lectures rarely work. [clinical opinion, weakly evidenced]
10. **Own whole zones, not shared tasks.** One person owns "laundry" end to end. Hand-offs mid-task
    drop things. [popular claim]
11. **Describe, never judge.** Say "the mail piles up on the counter", not "you never open mail".
12. **Check in weekly, 10 minutes, same time.** Adjust the system, not the person.

## Session script: opening with a parent who has ADHD

> "I'm not here to make this look a certain way. I'm here to find what makes the next five minutes
> easier. Tell me about the last time this worked, even for a day. What got in the way after?"

Then: pick ONE pain point (keys, mail, school bags). Observe the whole chain. Remove one step.
Test it this week. Review next week. Never start with a full-house plan.

## Sizing a task (use this exact procedure)

1. Name the finish line as a visible state ("counter clear", not "organize kitchen").
2. Cut into steps that take 5-15 minutes each; if the person hesitates, cut smaller.
3. Estimate time, then multiply by 1.5-2 and add buffer. Time is routinely underestimated;
   ICD advises adding buffer time. [professional practice]
4. Pick a container for the work: one timer, one zone, one bag/bin.
5. Define "stop": a timer ends the session even mid-job. Leaving a sticky note at the stopping point
   ("next: shelf 2") makes restarting easy.
6. Pair with a reward or a person (body double, music, podcast).

| Size | Time | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spark | 2 min | Put every cup in the sink |
| Sprint | 10-15 min | Clear the entry bench |
| Block | 30-45 min with a break | One drawer, with sort bins |
| Project | Scheduled over weeks | Garage, paperwork backlog; needs a body double |

## Low-energy days (a pre-agreed protocol)

Agree it on a GOOD day, write it on a card, put it on the fridge. [popular claim; sources were
app and coaching blogs, not clinical]

- **Floor tasks only:** meds/water/food, dishes for today, one pile of "act now" papers into a tray.
- **Everything else is paused, not failed.** A missed stretch does not erase earlier progress.
- **Ask for the smallest help:** "Can you sit with me for 10 minutes?" is an allowed sentence.
- **Lower the bar publicly:** paper plates, takeout, laundry in a basket, no guilt.
- If low energy lasts weeks, or mood seems very low, suggest talking with their doctor or
  therapist. Do not interpret it. [professional practice]

## Language: shame-free coaching

Avoid: "just", "again", "obviously", "lazy", "you need to try harder", "why can't you".
Prefer: "this system isn't working for you yet", "what would make that step lighter?",
"that's a design problem, not a character problem".

ICD's communication fact sheet lists what does not work (criticism, lecturing, policing, nagging,
exaggerating consequences, "just do it") and what does (curiosity, compassion, listening without
judgment, collaboration, noticing small wins). [professional practice]

Tuckman cautions against calling ADHD a gift as a blanket message; value strengths, but still
build supports and accommodations. [clinical]

## How adults with ADHD actually organize best (evidence map)

| Idea | Source type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Disorder of performance not knowledge; support at point of performance | [clinical] Barkley (secondary sources) | Why reminders must be physical and local |
| Time blindness: externalize time with clocks, timers, countdowns | [clinical] via ADDitude/Barkley | ICD also recommends analog clocks |
| Externalize memory: cards, notes, lists, apps | [clinical] ADDitude | Place at the point of use |
| Interest-based motivation (interesting, competitive, novel, urgent) | [clinical opinion] Dodson | Clinical experience, no cited trial |
| Open/visible storage; fewer clothes; one calendar; weekly admin hour | [professional practice] Pinsky (via summary) | Function over looks |
| "Hot spot" folder, launch pad by the door, 5-item to-do list | [professional practice] Kolberg via ADDitude | |
| Body doubling | [research: limited], [popular claim] | One small lab study found no measurable difference; people still liked it |
| Medication alone does not teach skills | [clinical] Tuckman | Skills and accommodations still needed |

Caveat: figures such as "30-40% less clutter" circulate without a traceable study. Never quote them.

## Systems that survive inconsistency

- **Default location for everything used daily.** Keys, wallet, glasses, charger: one landing
  spot, no lid. [professional practice]
- **Launch pad** by the exit: bags, shoes, school forms, tomorrow's things. Prep the night before.
- **Broad categories** for paper (3-6 folders) beat many narrow ones.
- **Automate**: bill pay, grocery delivery, recurring calendar entries, scheduled declutter slots.
- **"Ripening" spot** for undecided small items with a date: if untouched by the date, it goes.
- **Reset ritual** (10 min, same trigger daily, e.g. after dinner), done by whoever is home, timer on.
- **When a system dies, redesign it; do not restart it.** Ask: which step stopped happening? Remove or
  move that step.
- **One thing at a time.** Finish or consciously park a project before the next one.


## Couples, teens and children
Who owns which space and task, how to fade support with teens, and structured supports, safety and
caregiver load for younger children with significant needs: see `references/households-and-children.md`.
Short version: divide by whole zones and preference, move reminders from voice to visible, teens
own their room's layout while parents own shared spaces, hygiene and safety, and for children with
support needs ask the family's OT or therapist before buying anything.

## Things the organizer should do in every session
1. Ask what would make the next week easier.
2. Observe one chain of behavior.
3. Change one step.
4. Write the agreement on one card, in the person's words.
5. Book the next check-in before leaving.
6. Name a win.

## Red flags to hand off (do not diagnose)
Suggest a clinician when: low mood or hopelessness that persists, safety risks (fire, wandering,
medication), sleep collapse, or distress at discarding that exceeds the objects. For heavy clutter
and hoarding signs, load the `clutter-feelings-and-hoarding` skill.

## Pitfalls
- Starting with a full-house plan instead of one pain point and one changed step.
- Buying containers before reducing stuff; adding lids, folders and steps.
- Restarting a dead system instead of asking which step stopped happening.
- Quoting untraceable figures ("30-40% less clutter") or promising body doubling works.
- Shame words ("just", "again", "lazy"), lectures, or calling ADHD a gift as a blanket message.
- Interpreting mood, sleep or behavior yourself instead of suggesting a clinician.

## Verification
- The agreement for this week fits on one card, in the person's words, with a minimum version.
- Steps from "done using it" to "put away" were counted and are one or two.
- A check-in is booked and a win was named.
- Any safety item (exits, medications, wandering risk) was handled or referred.

## Household specifics
This skill is the method only. The agent's own local skill or the household's shared data docs
supply who lives in the home, who owns which zones, agreed routines and check-in times, and any
care-team guidance. Where they conflict with this skill, the household's rules win.
