# Customer discovery (The Mom Test method)

Sources:
- [MT] Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test (book site): https://www.momtestbook.com/
- [YC-T] Eric Migicovsky, "How to Talk to Users", YC Startup Library: https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6g-how-to-talk-to-users
- [NA] Noah Ahmed, summary of The Mom Test (practitioner notes): https://noahahmed.substack.com/p/summary-the-mom-test
- [PG-DS] Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale": https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

## The three rules [MT][YC-T][NA]
1. Talk about their life instead of your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.
YC teaches the same three errors: pitching the idea, asking hypotheticals, and accepting bad data [YC-T].
YC: only two things matter early, build the product and talk to users, and founders (including the developer) must do it themselves, not delegate it [YC-T].

## Bad data to discard or redirect [YC-T][NA]
| Bad data | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Compliments | "I love it", "this is really useful" | Deflect; ask about their last real instance of the problem |
| Fluff (generic, hypothetical, future) | "I would definitely...", "I usually..." | Anchor to a specific past event: "when was the last time?" |
| Feature requests / ideas | "You should add X" | Ask "why do you want that? what would it let you do?" |

## Question bank [NA]
- "Talk me through the last time that happened."
- "Why do you bother?" (moves from perceived to real problem)
- "What are the implications of that?" (separates pain from mild annoyance)
- "What else have you tried?" (if they never looked for a fix, they will not buy yours)
- "Why do you want that feature? What would that let you do?"
- "Who else should I talk to?" (end every conversation with this)
Add for money (working rule, consistent with rule 2): "What do you spend on this now, in money or hours?"

Never ask: "Do you think it's a good idea?", "Would you buy a product that did X?", "How much would you pay?"

## Commitment and advancement [NA]
- A meeting succeeds only if it ends with a commitment to a next step; there is no meeting that "went well".
- Commitment currencies: time, reputation risk, money. The more they give up, the more you can believe them.
- Ladder used here (lowest to highest; ordering is my working synthesis):
  1. Agrees to a follow-up conversation at a set time (time)
  2. Tries the product or a manual version with their real data (time)
  3. Introduces you to a colleague or decision-maker (reputation)
  4. Pre-orders, pays a deposit, or signs a letter of intent (money/reputation)
  5. Pays full price (money)
- Casual, short conversations work; it takes about five minutes to learn whether a problem exists and matters [NA].

## Segments
If answers are scattered, the segment is too broad: narrow to a who-and-where pair (a specific kind of person you can find in a specific place) [NA, "customer slicing"].

## Where to find the first people [PG-DS]
- Recruit users manually; nearly all startups have to. You cannot wait for them to come [PG-DS].
- Go where the segment already gathers and is reachable by you; an idea where you have no way to find users to recruit manually "is probably a bad idea, at least for those founders" [PG-DS, note 12].
- Set them up yourself ("Collison installation": set up the user on the spot rather than sending a link) [PG-DS].
- Written channels work: community forums, email, direct messages, existing user lists, mailing lists, online groups of the segment.
