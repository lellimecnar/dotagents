# Methods: turning a raw "crazy" idea into something that works and pays

Legend: **[S]** means the claim comes from the linked source. **[Syn]** means it is my synthesis or judgement. All sources were fetched on 2026-11-22.

---

## A. Finding the real goal behind an idea

- **Jobs-to-be-Done (Christensen).** **[S]** People "hire" a product to make progress on a job. Jobs can be small ("pass the time") or large ("find a more fulfilling career"). Christensen argues that innovation is hit-or-miss because firms study customer profiles and data correlations instead of the job. https://hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=51553
- **Outcome-Driven Innovation (Ulwick).** **[S]** A need is written as a "desired outcome statement": a metric customers use to judge success at a job. One job often has more than 100 of them. The method grew out of six-sigma metric thinking, and it starts by defining the job and drawing a job map. https://strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation/understanding-customer-needs
- **Four Forces (Moesta / Re-Wired).** **[S]** A customer switches only when Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit. https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces
- **5 Whys.** **[S]** This is Toyota/Ohno's iterative method for finding a root cause. https://lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/five-whys-animation
- **Laddering.** **[S]** Laddering comes from means-end theory. Interviews move from attributes to consequences to personal values (Reynolds & Gutman, *J. Advertising Research*, 1988). https://is.muni.cz/el/econ/jaro2013/MPH_MVPS/39278324/LadderingTheoy_original.pdf
- **Reframing (Wedell-Wedellsborg, HBR 2017).** **[S]**
  - In his survey of 106 C-suite executives, 85% agreed their organisations were bad at diagnosing problems, and 87% said this was costly.
  - His seven practices: establish legitimacy; bring in outsiders; get people's definitions in writing; ask what's missing; consider multiple categories; analyse positive exceptions ("bright spots"); question the objective.
  - Canonical example: tenants complain the elevator is slow. Installing mirrors fixed the complaints without speeding up the elevator.
  - https://hbr.org/2017/01/are-you-solving-the-right-problems (full text via https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://hbr.org/2017/01/are-you-solving-the-right-problems)
- **Working Backwards PR/FAQ (Amazon).** **[S]** Write the press release and FAQ before building anything. Most major Amazon initiatives since 2004 started this way. The narrative forces clarity about what to build. https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process
- **First principles.** **[S]** Separate the fundamental truths from inherited assumptions, then rebuild from those truths. https://fs.blog/first-principles
- **Outcome vs output.** **[S]** An output is the thing you ship. An outcome is a change in customer behaviour that drives business impact (Seiden). https://intercom.com/blog/podcasts/josh-seiden-on-why-product-teams-should-focus-on-outcome-vs-output ; https://producttalk.org/outcomes-vs-outputs

**[Syn] How these compare:**
- Use 5 Whys and laddering on the *founder's own* idea to find the underlying goal, such as "earn independent income" or "fix X for people like me".
- Use JTBD and Four Forces on the *customer*.
- Use reframing to generate 3–5 alternative problem statements before committing to one.
- Use the PR/FAQ as the forcing artifact: if you can't write the headline and first FAQ, the goal isn't clear yet.
- Watch the failure mode of 5 Whys: it converges on a single cause. Reframing deliberately diverges, so the two balance each other.

## B. Prior art: has someone already done it?

**Patents (all free):**
- **[S]** USPTO Patent Public Search, plus the USPTO's guide to search strategy: https://ppubs.uspto.gov/basic ; https://uspto.gov/patents/search
- **[S]** Espacenet: more than 150 million documents worldwide, updated daily. https://epo.org/en/searching-for-patents/technical/espacenet
- **[S]** Google Patents: covers 100+ patent offices and also returns Google Scholar, Google Books and Prior Art Archive results. https://guides.library.unt.edu/google/patents
- **[S]** Lens.org: free, open patent and scholarly data that anyone can export anonymously. https://researchguides.library.syr.edu/lensguide/use
- **[S]** For example, [EPC Article 54](https://www.epo.org/en/legal/epc/2020/a54.html) covers public disclosures before filing and certain earlier-filed European applications; [Article 55](https://www.epo.org/en/legal/epc/2020/a55.html) gives narrow exceptions. Scope patent questions by jurisdiction and date. This sweep can surface leads; it does not establish patentability or freedom to operate.

**Products, startups and graveyards:**
- **[S]** YC company directory (filterable by industry and location): https://ycombinator.com/companies
- **[S]** Failory Startup Cemetery (120+ post-mortems): https://failory.com/cemetery
- **[S]** CB Insights (March 2026) identified 431 publicly reported VC-backed shutdowns since 2023. Among the 385 with identifiable reasons, 70% ran out of capital, 43% cited poor product-market fit, 29% timing and 19% unsustainable unit economics; reasons can overlap. Use these as prompts for investigation, not population failure rates. https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/
- **[S]** The earlier CB Insights study of 101 post-mortems found "no market need" in 42% of cases. https://s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/cbi-content/research-reports/The-20-Reasons-Startups-Fail.pdf
- **[S]** Kickstarter publishes live stats: 42.5% of all projects succeed. Failed campaigns are a free demand signal. https://web.archive.org/web/20251003065624id_/https:/www.kickstarter.com/help/stats
- **[Syn]** Also check Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, GitHub, app stores and the Crunchbase free tier. Searching "<problem> alternative" and reading reviews is often faster than any database.

**Academic:**
- **[S]** Semantic Scholar is free and open (from the Allen Institute for AI). https://semanticscholar.org/about/librarians
- **[Syn]** Google Scholar and arXiv are the default complements.

**How to read "someone already did this" [Syn, anchored in sources]:**
1. **Existing paying users are validation, not a veto.** Paul Graham's "tarpit" warning is about ideas that sound good and many have tried. Demand that has been proven by others is different. https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html
2. **Classify why earlier attempts died:** no need, timing, economics, or execution. CB Insights shows timing and economics are common causes **[S]**. Those two can be fixed by "why now"; "no need" usually cannot.
3. **Answer "why now".** **[S]** Sequoia: "Nature hates a vacuum—so why hasn't your solution been built before now?" https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan
4. **Treat saturation as a positioning problem, not automatic death.** Apply Blue Ocean's ERRC grid to the incumbents (section D).

## C. Constructive critique without killing the idea

- **Pre-mortem (Klein, HBR 2007).** **[S]** Imagine the project has already failed and list the reasons. Klein cites Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989): prospective hindsight increased correct identification of reasons by 30%. https://web.archive.org/web/20160125162917/https:/hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem
  - **Caveat [S]:** the 1989 abstract says *temporal perspective* had little influence, while *outcome certainty* ("it **did** happen") strongly shaped the explanations people gave. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.3960020103
  - **[Syn]** So phrase it as certain: "It is 12 months later and this failed. Why?" Don't phrase it as "What could go wrong?". The 30% figure is often repeated, but it comes from one study.
- **Outside view and reference-class forecasting.** **[S]** Lovallo & Kahneman found executives' forecasts are optimistic. The fix is to pick a class of analogous efforts, take its distribution of outcomes, and place your project within it. https://hbr.org/2003/07/delusions-of-success-how-optimism-undermines-executives-decisions ; https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/nehring/teaching/econ106/readings/Lovallo-Kahneman-Optimism-HBR%20July%202003.pdf
  - **[S]** Flyvbjerg's three steps: (1) choose a reference class; (2) establish its probability distribution; (3) compare your project against it. The American Planning Association endorsed the method in 2005. https://pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068
- **Base rates.** **[S]** For US employer establishments from 1994 to 2021, 67.9% survived 2 years, 49.2% survived 5 years and 33.8% survived 10 years (SBA Advocacy). https://advocacy.sba.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Frequently-Asked-Questions-About-Small-Business_2024-508.pdf
- **Steelmanning.** **[S]** Build the strongest version of the opposing argument before rebutting it (Volokh on interpretive charity). https://reason.com/volokh/2021/05/12/steelmanning-and-interpretive-charity
  - **[Syn]** Do it in both directions: steelman the idea first, then steelman the strongest objection.
- **Disney Dreamer / Realist / Critic.** **[S]** Animators described "three different Walts". Dilts formalised them as Dreamer, Realist and Critic, kept as separate phases because "a critic and a dreamer without a realist just become stuck in perpetual conflict". https://lateralaction.com/articles/walt-disney
- **Riskiest-assumption testing.** **[S]** Strategyzer's Assumptions Mapping sorts desirability, feasibility and viability hypotheses on two axes: importance and evidence. Test the important, no-evidence quadrant first. https://strategyzer.com/library/how-assumptions-mapping-can-focus-your-teams-on-running-experiments-that-matter
- **[Syn] On red-teaming and "yes-and".** "Yes-and" belongs in the Dreamer phase only. A red team is the Critic phase made adversarial. The rule that keeps ideas alive is **separating the phases in time** and ending every critique with "...so the cheapest test of that is ___", which turns an objection into an experiment.

## D. Making it work and making it profitable

**Canvases:**
- **[S]** Business Model Canvas: Osterwalder's 9 building blocks. https://strategyzer.com/library/what-is-a-business-model
- **[S]** Lean Canvas (Maurya) replaces some blocks with Problem, Solution, Key Metrics and Unfair Advantage. His reasoning: startups mostly fail by building the wrong thing, and few have a real unfair advantage on day one. https://ashmaurya.com/blog/why-lean-canvas-versus-business-model-canvas
- **[S]** Value Proposition Canvas: customer jobs, pains and gains, mapped to pain relievers and gain creators. https://assets.strategyzer.com/assets/resources/the-value-proposition-canvas-instruction-manual.pdf

**Customer evidence:**
- **[S]** The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick) is about learning from customers "when everyone is lying to you". https://momtestbook.com
- Its three rules, as commonly summarised: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future; talk less. https://willparkeranderson.substack.com/p/the-mom-test

**Unit economics:**
- **[S]** CAC payback = months of gross margin needed to recover acquisition cost. Kellogg's example: payback of 15 vs 30 months, and the 30-month company is much riskier. LTV/CAC is the summary metric. https://kellblog.com/the-ultimate-saas-metric-ltv-cac
- **[Syn]** For a bootstrapper, contribution margin per sale and payback in under about 3–6 months matter more than LTV, because you can't fund a long payback.

**Pricing:**
- **[S]** Van Westendorp asks four price questions (too expensive, too cheap, expensive, bargain) and finds an acceptable range where the curves cross. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp's_Price_Sensitivity_Meter
- **[S]** Ramanujam (Simon-Kucher): "Market and price, then design, then build… design the product around the price." His named failures are "feature shock" and "minivation" (underpricing real value). https://zuora.com/2016/11/14/design-product-around-price

**Market sizing:**
- **[S]** Bottom-up sizing starts from the number of reachable customers × purchase behaviour, then works through TAM, SAM and SOM. https://visible.vc/blog/bottom-up-market-sizing

**Pivots (Ries's 10, as summarised):**
- **[S]** Zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel, technology. A startup's runway is really "the number of pivots it can still make". https://forbes.com/sites/martinzwilling/2011/09/16/top-10-ways-entrepreneurs-pivot-a-lean-startup

**Blue Ocean four actions:**
- **[S]** Which industry factors should be eliminated, reduced well below standard, raised well above standard, or created? https://blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/four-actions-framework

**Bootstrapping:**
- **[S]** Rob Walling's "stair step": start with a simple one-time-sale product that needs a single traffic channel, *not* SaaS. Move to recurring revenue later. https://robwalling.com/essays/2015/03/26/the-stair-step-method-of-bootstrapping

**Licensing instead of building:**
- **[S]** Stephen Key: licensing is "far easier and less risky than starting a business". You can license without a patent, using a sell sheet. The key filter is whether the idea can be manufactured at a competitive price with existing machinery. https://inventright.com/the-definitive-guide-to-licensing-when-you-get-a-great-idea-what-do-you-do-next ; https://inventright.com/blog/is-your-product-idea-right-for-licensing-how-to-tell

**Solo founders (the evidence is mixed):**
- **[S]** Greenberg & Mollick studied Kickstarter-founded firms and found solo-founded companies survived longer and earned more revenue than team-founded ones. https://stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/faculty-research/venture-investors-take-note-solo-founders-outperform-teams
- **[S]** Paul Graham lists "single founder" as startup mistake #1. https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html
- **[S]** Stripe reported that solo founders formed 63% of C corporations incorporated through Atlas in Q2 2026 to date as of May 28. For its 2025 comparison, top-decile solo-founded Atlas startups had about 61× median first-six-month revenue. Neither figure describes all startups or founder income. https://stripe.com/blog/top-solo-founder-traits
- **[Syn]** A solo, low-cash founder should prefer ideas with a short path to first revenue, one channel, few dependencies, and options to license or sell services.

## E. Why "everyone said it would never work" ideas sometimes win

- **[S]** Andreessen frames venture outcomes as a 2×2 of consensus/non-consensus × right/wrong. The money is made in "non-consensus and right", which "in practical terms… translates to crazy". https://a16z.com/12-things-i-learned-from-marc-andreessen
- **[S]** a16z also hosted the counterpoint, that being too far outside consensus carries its own risk. https://a16z.substack.com/p/is-non-consensus-investing-overrated
- **[S]** Thiel's question: "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" A secret is a truth that is unknown but discoverable. https://businessinsider.com/peter-thiel-how-to-create-innovative-companies-2014-9
- **[S]** Paul Graham's "schlep blindness": people unconsciously avoid ideas that involve painful work. Stripe is his example; payments were an obvious pain for a decade. https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html
- **[S]** Graham again: "Live in the future, then build what's missing." Good ideas are "noticed", not "thought up", and start with a narrow, deep "well" of users who urgently need the thing. https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html

**[Syn] Telling non-consensus-and-right apart from just wrong.** "Crazy" is only worth keeping if three conditions hold:
1. A specific small group already has the problem badly, with past-behaviour evidence.
2. There is a concrete why-now: a new technology, regulation, cost curve or behaviour.
3. The reason others missed it is a schlep, a secret, or a bad-timing graveyard, not "no need".

## Proposed method: the Idea Partner Loop (8 stages)

**Design rules [Syn]:**
- Process one idea at a time.
- Every stage produces a single artifact of one page or less.
- Use [the main skill's session and output rules](../SKILL.md) for selection, parking, permissions and verdicts. This worked example supplies optional techniques and timeboxes, not additional requirements.

### 0. Capture and park (10 min)
- **Purpose:** get the idea out of your head; choose which idea to work on now.
- **Questions:**
  - What is the idea in one sentence?
  - What excites you about it?
  - First screen against the founder's cash and time limits. When no active test would be displaced, optionally score energy, distinctive insight and affordability on 1–5 scales, with 5 always favorable. Use the score to discuss a choice, not to override the founder's priorities.
- **Tools:** current conversation or a founder-approved private parking list.
- **Artifact:** an idea card.
- **Exit:** one idea selected; the rest are parked.

### 1. Find the real goal (20 min)
- **Purpose:** separate the goal from the proposed solution.
- **Questions:**
  - Your goal: run 5 Whys or laddering. Is this about income, impact, curiosity, or scratching your own itch?
  - The customer's job: "When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___."
  - Reframing: write 3 alternative framings (what's missing? another category? a bright spot?).
  - What outcome would prove success, as opposed to the output you'd ship?
- **Tools:** JTBD, ODI outcome statements, Wedell-Wedellsborg's seven practices.
- **Artifact:** a goal statement plus 3 reframes, one of them chosen.
- **Exit:** you can name a specific person, the job they're trying to do, and a measurable outcome.

### 2. Working-backwards press release (20 min)
- **Purpose:** force clarity about what the product is.
- **Questions:**
  - Headline.
  - Who is the customer and what is the problem?
  - Why is this better than what they use today?
  - FAQ entries: price, why now, hardest objection.
- **Tools:** the Amazon PR/FAQ format.
- **Artifact:** a 1-page PR/FAQ.
- **Exit:** you can write it with no "TBD" in the customer, problem or price fields.

### 3. Prior-art sweep (60–90 min, timeboxed)
- **Purpose:** learn from people who tried before you, don't just get discouraged by them.
- **Questions:**
  - Who solves this today, including spreadsheets or doing nothing?
  - Who tried and died, and why: need, timing, economics or execution?
  - Are there patents that block this or could be licensed?
  - Why now?
- **Tools:**
  - Patents: Google Patents, Espacenet, Lens.org, USPTO PPubs.
  - Products: YC directory, Product Hunt, Failory, CB Insights, Kickstarter, app stores, GitHub.
  - Research: Semantic Scholar.
- **Artifact:** a 5–10 row competitor/graveyard table and a one-line why-now.
- **Exit:** classify the evidence as **no close analogue found / validated-but-beatable / saturated / prior failures**. For prior failures, distinguish documented causes from hypotheses and check whether they apply to the proposed variant. Pass unresolved risks to the critique stage rather than issuing a verdict from the classification alone.

### 4. Structured critique (30 min)
- **Purpose:** find the fatal flaws cheaply, without killing the idea's energy.
- **Order:**
  1. Steelman the idea (Dreamer).
  2. Pre-mortem with certainty framing (Critic): "It's 12 months on and it failed. Why?"
  3. Outside view: choose a comparable cohort and match its outcome and time horizon to the decision. Report a base rate only if the denominator is appropriate. Use shutdown-only or graveyard data to identify failure modes, not to estimate the probability of failure.
  4. Non-consensus check: what's the secret or the schlep?
- **Tools:** Klein's pre-mortem, Kahneman/Flyvbjerg reference-class forecasting, Disney roles.
- **Artifact:** the top 3 failure reasons, each rewritten as an assumption.
- **Exit:** every objection has been turned into a testable assumption.

### 5. Riskiest-assumption map → one verdict (15 min)
- **Purpose:** decide.
- **Questions:**
  - Sort the assumptions (desirability, viability, feasibility) on importance × evidence.
  - Which single assumption, if false, kills the idea?
- **Tools:** Strategyzer Assumptions Mapping.
- **Artifact:** **a verdict plus the single riskiest assumption.**
- **Exit:** identify the riskiest unresolved assumption and use the main skill's verdict list. If evidence falsifies the current version, check variants before recommending KILL. Treat manufacturability as one licensing screen; if counterparty interest or viable deal terms are unknown, TEST the licensing path rather than recommending a deal.

### 6. Cheapest next experiment (design 15 min; run over days)
- **Purpose:** get real evidence for under $100 and within about a week.
- **Options:**
  - 5 Mom-Test interviews about past behaviour.
  - A landing page or pre-order with a price on it.
  - A concierge or manual version of the service.
  - A licensing sell sheet.
- **Set thresholds before running:** name the target segment, recruitment channel, minimum qualified exposure, deadline and pass/fail criteria. If reach is too low or the sample is off-target, mark the result inconclusive. A handful of interviews can screen for workarounds; it does not establish willingness to pay.
- **Tools:** The Mom Test, Testing Business Ideas experiment cards.
- **Artifact:** a test card with hypothesis, audience, metric, thresholds, deadline and owner.
- **Exit:** deliver the card for a design-only request. If execution was authorized, record the denominator and outcome against the thresholds.

### 7. Money model (30 min, only after a passed desirability test)
- **Purpose:** show the idea can be profitable before building more.
- **Questions:**
  - What is willingness to pay? Use Van Westendorp or direct price asks, and design the product around the price.
  - Unit economics: contribution margin per sale, a realistic CAC for one channel, payback in months.
  - Bottom-up SOM: reachable customers × price × conversion.
  - Blue Ocean ERRC against incumbents.
  - Revenue model: one-time sale → recurring (stair step), license, or services.
- **Tools:** Lean Canvas, Value Proposition Canvas, Ramanujam, Walling.
- **Artifact:** a Lean Canvas and a 10-line unit-economics table.
- **Exit:** convert the obtainable sales estimate into a cash-flow forecast over the same period as the founder's goal. Subtract fulfillment, acquisition, overhead and other relevant costs without double-counting. Check that cash does not run out before payback and that a plausible scenario supports the required founder income. If the numbers fail, pick one pivot; if key inputs are unknown, test those before recommending BUILD.

### 8. Build or persevere review (recurring, weekly or each sprint)
- **Purpose:** make continuing a decision, not a drift.
- **Questions:**
  - Did the metric move?
  - Persevere, pivot (choose from the 10 types), or kill?
- **Artifact:** a one-line decision log.
- **Exit:** a dated decision. Past 3 failed tests on the same assumption, PIVOT or PARK by default.

**Agent output:** use the [main skill's output shape](../SKILL.md). The test card above is an optional artifact for a useful, authorized next experiment, not a required closing format.

**Limits:**
- HBR full texts were read through archive copies.
- The 30% pre-mortem figure comes from a single 1989 study, and that paper's own abstract attributes the effect to certainty rather than to time perspective.
- The solo-founder evidence conflicts across sources.
- Base rates are for US employer establishments, not venture startups.
- Crunchbase, Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, Testing Business Ideas and Monetizing Innovation weren't fetched first-hand beyond the pages cited; Monetizing Innovation is covered through an author interview.
