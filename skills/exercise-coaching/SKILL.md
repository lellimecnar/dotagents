# Exercise coaching

Expert method for exercise programming and coaching for adults at beginner to intermediate levels, adapted to their schedules. This skill supplies the method and decision rules. Use reputable reference sources or calculators when useful; no companion skill is required. Every number here is sourced in `references/` (source list: `references/sources.md`).

**Volatile facts are looked up, never recalled:** screening-form editions, activity and dietary guideline editions, crisis-line channels, online tools, game settings and device-accuracy claims. State the edition and the date checked; edition-labelled numbers are illustrations.

## When to use
Any task that asks for a workout, routine, exercise advice, progress review, recovery/sleep-and-exercise question, or nutrition targets tied to training or body composition.

## The method (do these in order)

### 1. Screen before you prescribe (`references/screening-and-red-flags.md`)
- Check for **emergency signs first**: chest pain/pressure, pain spreading to arm/jaw/neck/back, unusual breathlessness, fainting, cold sweat, irregular heartbeat during or after exercise → stop exercise, tell the person to call emergency services now. Do not coach through it.
- Ask the person to complete the current official **PAR-Q+** self-screen at https://eparmedx.com/print-versions-of-par-q. All "no" → may start light-to-moderate and build gradually. Any "yes" → the person follows the current form's instructions, using ePARmed-X+ or consulting a clinician or qualified exercise professional as indicated. You never interpret a medical condition yourself.
- Apply the **ACSM 2015 logic** for intensity: (a) currently active? (b) known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease or symptoms? (c) desired intensity. Inactive + healthy + no symptoms → light-to-moderate without clearance. Inactive with known disease (no symptoms) or any symptoms → medical clearance before starting. Active with any signs/symptoms → stop exercising and get medical clearance before resuming at ANY intensity. Active, no symptoms, known stable disease → may continue light-to-moderate; clearance before vigorous (ACSM/Riebe 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26473759/).
- Re-screen when health changes, after illness, and at the validity or clearance interval the current PAR-Q+ edition states (check it at task time).

### 2. Find the real gaps
Read fixed commitments, energy patterns and existing habits from the calendar, notes and coordinator you are authorized to use; ask the person only for what none of them hold; do not guess. Build on what they already do (an active game, a walk, a sport) rather than replacing it. A plan that needs a slot that does not exist is a wrong plan.

### 3. Set the dose with FITT-VP (`references/program-design.md`)
- **Target:** Use current activity guidance for the person's location and population. For example, the U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines recommend for adults: 150–300 min/wk moderate or 75–150 min/wk vigorous aerobic (1 vigorous min ≈ 2 moderate), plus muscle-strengthening for all major groups on ≥2 days/wk. Any bout length counts.
- **Start below target** if inactive. The biggest gain is going from none to some; the ACSM 2026 resistance stand says the best program is the one the person will do.
- **Minimum effective dose** for time-poor people: 2 short full-body sessions/wk of multi-joint exercises; ≥4 hard sets per muscle group per week works; supersets roughly halve session time; warm up with lighter sets of the exercise itself.
- **Intensity** without equipment: talk test (moderate = can talk, not sing; vigorous = a few words only) or 0–10 effort (moderate 5–6, vigorous 7–8). For strength: novices perform 8–12 reps per set with a load that leaves about 2–4 reps in reserve (RIR), not taken to failure; novices are poor at judging RIR, so cross-check with "form still clean" and the top of the rep range (ACSM 2009 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/; Helms 2016 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/).

### 4. Choose exercises by movement pattern (`references/exercise-library.md`)
One each of squat, hinge, push, pull, plus a trunk/carry option. Start with the regression the person can do with clean form; progress to the next variation only when they hit the top of the rep range with good form. Give every exercise: setup, 2–3 execution cues, common faults, regression, progression, stop signs.

### 5. Plan progression and recovery
- Progress one variable at a time: reps → sets → harder variation/load (+2–10% when 1–2 reps over target on two sessions in a row) → frequency.
- Plan a lighter week when life spikes (illness, travel, poor sleep) rather than on a fixed calendar for novices; reduce sets, effort or frequency (deload consensus; the exact cut, e.g. half the sets, is coaching judgment) and keep the habit slot.
- Watch sleep: adults need ≥7 h; finish *vigorous* sessions more than 1 h before bed, preferably at least 2 h (sleep may be impaired when vigorous exercise ends ≤1 h before bed; Stutz 2019 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30374942/); moderate evening exercise does not harm sleep. Untreated sleep apnea makes drowsiness risky; exercise lowers apnea severity but never replaces prescribed therapy (`references/recovery-and-sleep.md`).

### 6. Design for adherence, especially ADHD (`references/adherence-and-adhd.md`)
- Few options, already chosen, each a concrete session (what, how long, where); never a start-up step.
- If-then plans tied to an existing cue (e.g. "after lunch, I walk for 20 minutes"; choose a cue from the person's actual routine). Planning interventions show small-to-medium effects; reinforcement helps.
- A **floor version** for bad days (one song, one set): missing a day does not break habit formation, but repeated misses lower it. Habits take ~2 months on average (range 18–254 days); exercise takes longer than simpler habits.
- Make it stimulating (music, game, novelty), visible progress, and social (body-doubling) when the person likes company; reduce setup friction (gear out the night before).

### 7. Provide nutrition targets only (`references/nutrition-targets-brief.md`)
Compute an energy range and protein/fiber targets, then give the **Nutrition targets brief** privately to the person. Share only the necessary targets with a meal planner if the person explicitly authorizes that handoff. Keep health data out of shared documents. This brief contains targets, not menus or recipes.

### 8. Deliver and follow up
One page: the week at a glance with each session named concretely, the three cues per exercise, the floor version, when to progress, stop signs. Review after 2–4 weeks: did it happen? Fix the schedule fit before changing the training.

## Stop and refer
| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Chest pain/pressure, fainting, severe breathlessness, signs of heart attack or stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty, sudden numbness/confusion/vision loss/severe headache) | Stop. Emergency services now (`references/screening-and-red-flags.md` §1). |
| Any PAR-Q+ initial "yes" | Person completes the follow-up specified by the current official form, using ePARmed-X+ as indicated. Follow-ups all "no" → start low-to-moderate and build gradually. Any follow-up "yes" → further advice from a clinician/qualified exercise professional before becoming more active. |
| Known heart/metabolic/kidney disease, new or any symptoms, pregnancy | Apply the ACSM branches in Method step 1: symptoms → stop and get clearance before any intensity; known disease without symptoms → clearance before vigorous (before starting if inactive); pregnancy → clinician or ePARmed-X+. |
| Sharp, joint-based, worsening or persistent pain; new swelling, numbness or tingling | Stop the provoking exercise and recommend assessment. After injury, numbness, tingling, deformity or a cold or discolored limb needs emergency assessment; severe or increasing swelling needs urgent care. Do not suggest a substitute that loads the affected area while red flags are unresolved. |
| Excessive daytime sleepiness, drowsy driving, CPAP intolerance | Sleep clinician; no driving while drowsy. |
| Disordered eating signs, very-low-calorie plans, medications affecting appetite/weight, diabetes | Physician/registered dietitian; you stay at general targets. |
| Low mood, hopelessness | Encourage contact with their clinician; crisis line if at risk (988 in the US, https://988lifeline.org). |

You coach healthy-adult exercise. You do not diagnose, treat injury, change medication or override a clinician.

## Common mistakes
- Writing the ideal program instead of the one that fits the real gaps; replacing an activity the person enjoys.
- Skipping screening, or the opposite: sending every healthy beginner to a doctor (ACSM 2015 removed risk-factor-based referral).
- Counting an exergame as moderate/vigorous without checking: the same game measures anywhere from very light to vigorous depending on level and player.
- Progressing on a calendar instead of on performance and form; adding several variables at once.
- Long lists and choice menus for someone with ADHD; vague sessions ("do some cardio"); no floor version.
- Adding start-up steps such as "put on shoes".
- Vigorous training right before bed; ignoring sleepiness.
- Restarting at full load after a break, or training hard through illness or a run of short nights.
- Reacting to one day of wearable data, using device calories or readiness scores as inputs, or reading a value medically.
- Treating a missing wearable data type as a signal; check which types the source returns and its latest data date first.
- Changing a plan without naming the rule behind the change.
- Giving menus instead of targets; putting health numbers in shared documents.
- Citing numbers from memory. Use `references/`; if a figure is not there, research and add it with a URL.
