## 2. Core principles (imperative + why)

**Time and estimates**
1. **Pad every estimate.** Use the brief's minutes as the base. Multiply by the person's calibration
   factor for that task kind if one exists (median actual/estimate from completion history, bounded
   0.7-3) [34]. With no history, use 1.5x for M-XL or unfamiliar tasks and 1.25x for XS-S routine.
   Why: people underestimate even their own familiar tasks. Thesis students predicted 34 days and
   took 55, and under a third finished by their own "best guess" [15]. ADHD adds measurable timing and
   estimation deficits (meta-analysis of 55 studies) [12][13]. The exact factor has *no* evidence base
   (the folk "3x rule" is a guess). The personal calibration factor is what matters, so replace the
   default once 3+ completions of a kind exist.
2. **Plan to the 15 minutes, never the minute.** Start and end on :00/:15/:30/:45. Precision nobody
   can deliver produces an instant "I'm already behind" failure.
3. **Externalize time.** Every box gets a visible start and end in the calendar. The description
   lists the concrete tasks by result, never start-up steps [1][3][9].

**Transitions and slack**
4. **Insert transition buffers.** 10 min between boxes that change context. 15 min when location
   or mental mode changes. Before any off-site fixed event: travel time + 10-20 min, plus a leave-by
   time in its prep [9][11][38]. Why: attention residue from the previous task degrades the next
   one, and it is worst when the prior task is unfinished [16]. ADHD transitions are a known failure point [6][7].
5. **Leave slack: book at most 60-70% of available hours** (available = awake hours minus fixed
   events, meals, routines and travel). Go lower when velocity says so (section 4). Why: queues and
   variable-duration work collapse near 100% utilization [30], and coaches prescribe white space for
   overruns and distraction [11]. 60-70% comes from practice and has not been tested in trials. Calibrate via velocity.
6. **Free time is not a box.** Do not create "free time", "rest" or "buffer" events as tasks.
   Slack is empty calendar space. Exception: a *specific* restorative activity the person chose
   (walk, nap, gym) may be a box, named concretely. Empty "rest" boxes get ignored or
   turn into guilt. Pacing works when rest is real and deliberate [37].
7. **Leave spillover room as empty slack** late in the day (20-45 min), never an Overflow box.
   Newport plans on revising the schedule when the day breaks, and this slack is the space for that [23][24].

**Load, energy, order**
8. **Put the hardest high-cognitive-load task in the person's focus window** (from data docs).
   If none is recorded, ask the coordinator for it; do not assume one. Tuckman:
   things planned earlier in the day are more likely to happen [5]. Chronotype/synchrony effects
   are real but modest and inconsistent in large tests [18][19]. Prefer the person's stated window
   and completion history over theory.
9. **Avoid high-load work in the post-lunch dip** (roughly 1-3 h after the midday meal, ~14:00-16:00
   by default) [17]. Use that time for low-load, physical, errand or social tasks.
10. **Never stack two high-load blocks back to back.** Put a low-load or physical box, or a 15+ min
    break, between them. Don't justify this with "decision fatigue / willpower depletion": the ego-depletion
    effect is tiny in multi-lab replications (d≈0.1) [20]. The real reasons are sustained-attention
    limits, time-blind overrun and residue [12][16].
11. **Start the day with an easy win**: one XS/S low-load task (5-15 min) before or at the start of
    the first work box. Why: initiation is the bottleneck. A fast completion builds momentum and
    gives the urgency/novelty hit the interest-based nervous system runs on [10][31][32]. (Coach
    consensus; weak formal evidence.)
12. **Cap box length.** High-load: 25-50 min of work, then a 5-10 min break inside or between boxes,
    with a max of 90 min including breaks. Low-load or physical: max 120 min. Split longer tasks into
    "(part 1/3)" boxes in different slots [34]. Why: sustained attention degrades, and timers and
    breaks externalize the passage of time [6][9].
13. **Vary the day.** Alternate kinds (desk / physical / out-of-house / social). Do not put three
    boxes of the same kind in a row unless they are a batch. Interest, novelty and challenge drive ADHD
    engagement, and monotony kills it [31][32]. (Practice-based.)
14. **Pair dreaded tasks** with a reward box after them, or with a body double (another person present
    or a co-working session) when the brief says one is available [7][27]. Evidence for body doubling
    is mostly self-report surveys from vendors [28][29], so treat it as an option, not a guarantee.

**Placement and selection**
15. **Anchor to fixed events first.** Place fixed events, the travel, prep and recovery around them,
    and meals and routines from data docs. Then fit tasks into the gaps [23][25].
16. **Deadline-first, then priority.** Order: hard deadline inside the horizon (work back from the
    deadline and leave at least one day of margin before it [25]) → bills/money due → brief priority
    → postponed count (higher first) → age. Mark deadline, bill and mandatory items "mandatory" and
    everything else "suggested" (format per the household's calendar conventions).
17. **Spread neglected tasks.** Any task with postponed count ≥2 gets a guaranteed slot early in
    the week, with no more than two such tasks per day (they are usually aversive [5]). At postponed
    ≥3, shrink it: schedule only its first 15-25 min step, and flag it in the output for the coordinator.
18. **Batch by location/context and batch group.** Group errands by area into one out-of-house box.
    Group same-tool tasks (admin, email, paperwork) into one box with checkboxes [11][33].
19. **Respect the household's rest-day rule** (from its data docs): move chore-type work earlier, never later.
20. **Money constraints.** Place bill-related tasks before due dates and after any stated pay date.
    Never schedule a purchase or errand the brief marks unaffordable or blocked.
21. **Plan the riskiest box with margin.** Leave empty slack after each day's riskiest box and
    choose its smaller version in advance; never write conditional "if ... then" text in a box.
    Planning the obstacle ahead (implementation intentions) has a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment across 94 studies [21] and help children with ADHD with
    shifting and distraction [22] (adult ADHD evidence is thinner).
22. **Fewer boxes when completion drops.** Velocity sets the total load (section 4). An under-filled
    plan that gets done beats a full plan that doesn't, and overplanning is the classic ADHD failure [10][11].
