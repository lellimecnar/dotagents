# Research and fact-checking method

Use this method for research and news analysis within the task’s authorized scope. Use the requester’s applicable report format, contact preferences and authorization constraints. Use an available citation workflow and authorized alternatives for inaccessible sources. Details, tables and every source URL are in `references/`.

## When to Use
Any research report, option comparison, fact-check, consumer purchase question or news brief. For a
one-fact lookup use only Step 1's depth rule and the citation rule; skip the rest.

## Step 1: Take bearings before reading anything
- Write the decision in one line: who decides, what they will do with the answer, what would change it.
- Classify the question; it picks the evidence you need (references/evidence-and-statistics.md):
  how common / does it work / what are the harms / is this test accurate / which product / what happened.
- Set the depth on purpose. SIFT's Stop move: a quick check of who is behind a source is often enough
  for a casual question; deep work chases each claim (Caulfield). Quick lookups stay quick.
- Skim the result list before clicking (click restraint) and plan which source types you need. Fact
  checkers who did this reached better conclusions in a fraction of the time (Wineburg & McGrew).

## Step 2: Find and vet sources (SIFT + lateral reading)
Follow references/source-evaluation.md (summary below):
1. Investigate the source by leaving it: search the outlet, author or organisation in new tabs and read
   what others say about it. Do not judge a site by its own About page, logo, design or .org domain.
2. Find better coverage: if the claim matters, look for the best source on it, not this source.
3. Trace claims, quotes, numbers and media to the original context; cite the origin, not the echo.
4. Rank: primary (law, filing, dataset, spec, study) > independent expert synthesis > reporting >
   reviews > forums (colour only, labelled). Note every date.
5. Two outlets repeating one origin are one source. Corroboration means independent origins.

## Step 3: Weigh the evidence
- Use a body-of-evidence judgement, not a single study. A good systematic review beats any one trial.
- Start where GRADE starts: randomised trials begin high, observational studies begin low. Rate down for
  risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias; rate observational
  evidence up only for a large effect, a dose-response gradient, or confounding that would shrink the
  effect. End at high / moderate / low / very low certainty.
- Match the design to the question (OCEBM): trials for "does it work"; surveys for "how common";
  cross-sectional accuracy studies for tests; mechanism-based reasoning is the lowest rung everywhere.
- Sources disagree on pyramids: some library guides put guidelines on top. Follow GRADE and OCEBM,
  because design is only the starting point and quality can move a study up or down.

## Step 4: Read the numbers critically
Apply references/evidence-and-statistics.md every time a number carries the conclusion:
- Convert relative risk to absolute risk with the baseline ("20% lower" might be 5 to 4 in 1,000).
- Use natural frequencies for tests and screening (out of 1,000 people, how many test positive, how
  many of those truly have it). Base rates dominate when the condition is rare.
- Prefer mortality to survival rates when judging screening; survival rises with earlier diagnosis
  even when nobody lives longer.
- A p-value is not the chance the claim is true and not the size of the effect. Report the effect and its
  range; ask whether the range includes both "no effect" and "important effect".
- Ask: compared with what, over what time, in whom, who paid, how many people or events.

## Step 5: Consumer comparisons
Follow references/consumer-research.md:
- Decide the criteria that matter to this household before looking at products.
- Prefer primary data (spec sheets, EnergyGuide labels, ENERGY STAR and regulatory listings, recall
  databases, warranty terms) and independent lab testing over star ratings. Star averages track
  lab-tested quality poorly and are pushed up by price and brand.
- Read reviews for failure patterns and dates, not averages; check sample size and incentives.
- Compare total cost of ownership (price + install + running cost x years - incentives that still
  exist). Re-check every incentive, rebate and tax credit against the agency page on the day you write.
- Prepare the comparison and name a lower-cost option. Leave the purchase to the authorized decision-maker.

## Step 6: News analysis
Follow references/news-analysis.md:
- Get the primary document for the story type (bill text, filing, transcript, dataset, release).
- Label each piece: news, analysis, opinion, sponsored. Check the label against the content.
- Compare outlets that lean different ways; list what each stresses and omits.
- Anonymous sourcing: apply AP's three tests (information not opinion, unavailable otherwise, source in a
  position to know) and say when a claim rests only on unnamed sources.
- Media bias charts rate outlets, not articles. Use them for orientation only, never as a verdict.
- Verdicts use the most apt word the facts support: false, misleading, exaggerated, missing context,
  unproven, accurate. If you cannot confirm, say so and say what you tried.

## Step 7: Write it up with calibrated confidence
Follow references/report-writing-confidence.md and the report layout (Answer, Options compared,
Evidence, Risks and tradeoffs, Limits, Sources). The bar behind it is `due-diligence-before-deciding`:
- Compare the alternatives before calling one best; a ranked pick names what it beat and why.
- An unverified fact never carries a recommendation: choose among what you could verify, or give a
  conditional answer whose branches are each verified.
- Limits holds only what no available source could settle after you tried every authoritative route:
  say what you tried, and why the recommendation does not depend on it.
- Confidence (high/medium/low) is about the evidence behind a judgement. Likelihood (likely, unlikely)
  is about the event. Do not mix both in one sentence.
- High: several independent, good sources agree, few assumptions. Medium: credible but thin or one key
  assumption. Low: fragmentary, conflicting, or rests on assumptions.
- Name what would change the answer. Put the strongest counter-evidence in the report.
- Give the first physical action and few options, already ranked, unless the requester asks otherwise.

## When to stop and refer out
Research informs; it does not replace a licensed professional or an emergency service. Details and
sources: references/referral-and-safety.md (routes table). Stop and say so plainly when:
- Someone may be in danger now: emergency services (911 in the US; text 911 only where offered, call if
  you can). Crisis or suicidal thoughts: 988 by call, text or chat at 988lifeline.org.
- Possible poisoning with no life-threatening symptoms: webPOISONCONTROL online triage, which itself
  says when a poison centre or ER is needed. Collapse, seizure, trouble breathing: 911.
- A diagnosis, dosing, legal advice on a live matter, tax filing positions, structural, gas or electrical
  work, or anything a licence exists to protect: give the evidence summary, then name the kind of
  professional to consult and a verified, accessible local contact route.
- Respect the person’s stated contact preferences when safe and supported; say when no suitable alternative exists.

## Common mistakes (check before sending)
- Reading vertically: judging a site from inside itself.
- Citing the echo (a news story about a study) instead of the study.
- Treating two copies of one press release as two sources.
- Reporting a relative risk without the baseline, or a survival rate as proof screening works.
- Trusting a 4.6-star average from 40 reviews; ignoring review dates and incentives.
- Quoting an incentive, price or rule from an undated or stale page (agency fact sheets lag the law).
- Using a bias chart as a fact-check; calling a disputed story "fake news".
- Stating confidence without saying what drives it or what would change it.
- Search snippets cited as if the page was read. Fetch the page.
- Padding: if you found nothing, say so.

## Pitfall: citation ledger
Use a citation ledger if one is available in the current environment. If it is not reachable, number sources by hand strictly from fetched URLs, never from
memory, and re-check every number against the Sources list before delivering.
