# News analysis: primary documents, labels, bias tools, verdicts

## Primary document by story type
| Story type | Primary source to get | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Federal bill or law | Bill text and status; enacted public law | congress.gov; govinfo.gov |
| Federal rule or regulation | Final rule text, effective and compliance dates | federalregister.gov; ecfr.gov |
| Court case | Docket, opinion, filing | Court site; supremecourt.gov; PACER/CourtListener |
| Speech, debate, hearing | Full transcript or video, not the clip | Official site, C-SPAN, committee site |
| Economic or health statistic | The dataset and its release notes | BLS, Census, BEA, CDC, agency site |
| Study | The paper (abstract, methods, limitations, funding) | Journal, PubMed, preprint server (label preprints) |
| Company news | Press release, SEC filing | Company site, sec.gov EDGAR |
| State or local | Agenda, minutes, ordinance text, agency notice | City/county/state sites |
(Locations are standard official repositories; confirm the exact page each time.)

## Separate reporting from opinion
The Trust Project's eight indicators: best practices (standards, mission, funding), journalist expertise,
labels (news, opinion, analysis, sponsored), references (sources for each claim), methods, locally
sourced, diverse voices, actionable feedback (corrections). [1] Use "Labels" and "References" on every
story:
- Is it labelled news, analysis, opinion or sponsored? Does the content match the label?
- Does each contested claim carry attribution? AP: "Anything in the AP news report that could reasonably
  be disputed should be attributed." [2]
- Flag loaded verbs ("slammed", "admitted"), headlines stronger than the body, and missing base rates or
  time frames.

## Anonymous sources (AP standard)
Read the wire service's current news-values page for its attribution and anonymous-source rules before
applying them; the three tests below are its 2024 wording.
AP uses anonymous material only if: (1) it is information, not opinion or speculation, and vital to the
report; (2) it is not available except under anonymity; (3) the source is reliable and in a position to
have direct knowledge. AP routinely requires more than one source, must say why anonymity was granted, and
treats other outlets' anonymous reports with "the most careful scrutiny," trying to confirm them
independently before use. [2]
In briefs: say "rests on unnamed sources" whenever a claim does, note whether any outlet confirmed it
independently, and never present unnamed-source opinion as fact.

## Media bias and reliability ratings: uses and limits
Raters revise their methods: read each rater's current methodology page before describing it; the method
details below are as published at time of writing.
- AllSides rates political lean only, in five buckets, using blind bias surveys and multipartisan
  editorial reviews; panels of six to nine reviewers assign -6 to +6. [3][4]
- Ad Fontes rates both bias and reliability, each article by at least three analysts with balanced
  left/center/right self-reported politics. [5]
- Experts quoted by Poynter praised AllSides' and Ad Fontes' stated methods but noted neither explicitly
  rates editorial standards. [4]
- A 2023 PNAS Nexus study of six expert rating sets (NewsGuard, MBFC, Ad Fontes, fact-checkers, Iffy,
  Lasser et al.) found they "generally correlated highly" at the domain level, but their scales are not
  commensurate: 80% of fact-checker-rated domains fell below 0.60 versus 29% of Ad Fontes domains. [5]
Limits I apply:
1. Ratings describe outlets, not the article in front of me. A reliable outlet can run a bad story.
2. Lean is not accuracy. Do not treat "center" as "true".
3. Thresholds and scales differ between raters; never compare scores across raters.
4. Use them to pick a spread of outlets to compare, not to settle a fact. The primary document settles it.

## Verdict wording (AP Stylebook fact-check guidance)
From AP's 2017 "fact checks, fake news" entry (Stylebook is paywalled; text as quoted by Poynter and
other outlets): [6][7]
- Present the exact assertion, quickly state what is wrong or right, then the facts with citations.
- Check facts, not opinions or tastes.
- Statements need not be clearly true or false: "true, false, exaggerated, a stretch, a selective use of
  data, partly or mostly true" — use the most apt description the facts support.
- If a statement cannot be confirmed, say so (later versions add: describe the efforts made). [8]
- Do not label individual disputed items "fake news"; say false, erroneous, unverified, questionable or
  disputed, as appropriate.

## Brief template (fits a standard news method)
1. What happened (one or two sentences, with the primary document linked).
2. Why it matters to the reader.
3. Coverage compared: outlet A stresses..., outlet B stresses..., both omit...
4. Spin flagged: loaded wording, unsupported headline, missing context, unnamed-source-only claims.
5. What is still unknown or disputed, who disputes it, on what evidence.
6. Confidence and what would change it.
