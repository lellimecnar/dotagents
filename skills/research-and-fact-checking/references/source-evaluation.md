# Source evaluation: SIFT, lateral reading, tracing, ranking

## SIFT (Mike Caulfield)
The four moves: Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims, quotes and media to the
original context. [1]
- **Stop** has two jobs: before reading or sharing, ask whether you know the source and its reputation;
  and mid-search, stop when you are going down a rabbit hole and recall your purpose. For a casual
  question, knowing whether the publication is reputable is "probably good enough"; deep research
  chases individual claims. Both depths are legitimate. [1]
- **Investigate the source**: know expertise and agenda before reading (Caulfield's examples: a Nobel
  economist on economics; the dairy industry on milk). That does not make either right or wrong; it
  changes how you interpret them. Taking about sixty seconds to find out where media comes from is the
  suggested budget. [1]
- **Find better coverage**: when the claim matters more than the source, look for the best reporting or
  analysis on the claim itself (consensus, fact-checks, primary documents).
- **Trace to the original**: quotes, statistics, images and video lose context as they are passed on.
  Find the first appearance and read it in context.

## Lateral reading (Stanford History Education Group)
Study: 10 PhD historians, 10 professional fact checkers and 25 Stanford undergraduates evaluated live
websites. Historians and students were fooled by "official-looking logos and domain names" and read
vertically (stayed on the site). Fact checkers read laterally, "leaving a site after a quick scan and
opening up new browser tabs", and "arrived at more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time." [2]
The three fact-checker practices: [2]
| Practice | What it means | What I do |
|---|---|---|
| Taking bearings | Chart a plan before diving into unfamiliar content | Write the decision and the source types needed first |
| Lateral reading | Leave the site; open new tabs to judge it from outside | Search the org/author name + "funding", "criticism", Wikipedia, news coverage |
| Click restraint | Scan the whole results page before clicking | Read the result list; prefer the source that is best, not first |
Weak heuristics (domain .org, logos, About page, look and feel) are "widely used but flawed"; strong
heuristics like lateral reading "save time" and are more accurate. [2]
Teachability: in an online college course (n = 87, four one-hour modules), students used lateral reading
more after instruction and improved significantly; the modules explicitly corrected the misconception
that a dot-org domain makes a site trustworthy. [3]

## Source ranking (made operational)
| Rank | Type | Examples | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Primary | statute, rule text (Federal Register/eCFR), court filing, dataset, peer-reviewed study, manufacturer spec, agency listing | Read the actual section; note version/date; agency summaries can lag the law |
| 2 | Independent expert synthesis | systematic review, Cochrane, guideline built on GRADE, independent lab testing | Funding, conflicts, date |
| 3 | Reporting and reviews | wire services, major outlets, trade press, professional reviewers | Is it tracing to a primary? Business model (affiliate, free samples) |
| 4 | Forums, anecdotes | Reddit, owner groups, comment threads | Colour only, label as "users report"; useful for failure patterns |

## Corroboration rules
- Independent means a separate origin, not a separate URL. Syndicated copies and articles rewriting one
  press release count once.
- When sources disagree, present both with citations and say which you weight and why.
- AP's own bar for anonymous material: more than one source is routinely required; a single source only
  when an authoritative figure gives detail "so detailed that there is no question of its accuracy." [4]

## What good fact-checkers disclose (IFCN Code of Principles)
Use these five commitments as a checklist when judging a fact-checking outlet, and as the standard for
my own reports: [5]
1. Non-partisanship and fairness: same standard for every claim regardless of who made it; selection not
   concentrated on one side; disclose sources' interests.
2. Standards and transparency of sources: enough detail that readers can replicate the work.
3. Transparency of funding and organisation (signatories list funders of 5% or more of revenue). [6]
4. Standards and transparency of methodology: set out evidence that supports the claim and evidence that
   undermines it; apply the same standard to equivalent claims.
5. Open and honest corrections policy: correct visibly so readers see the corrected version.

## Quick lateral-reading script (2 to 5 minutes)
1. Who runs it? Search the domain or org name in a new tab; read Wikipedia and independent coverage.
2. Who funds it? Search "<org> funding" / "<org> donors"; check whether it is an industry or advocacy group.
3. Is the author a real expert in this topic? Search name + field.
4. Does anyone credible cite or dispute this specific claim? Search the claim in quotes.
5. Where did the number, quote or image first appear? Follow links back; reverse-image search media.
6. Decide: use as primary / use with attribution / colour only / discard. Write the reason in one line.
